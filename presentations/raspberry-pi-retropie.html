What Is a Raspberry Pi?

A complete computer on a single board about the size of a credit card. It runs a full Linux operating system, plugs into any monitor or TV, and has a row of pins that let code control real-world electronics. The UK-based Raspberry Pi Foundation created it in 2012 specifically to get students programming, and more than 60 million have been sold since.

Raspberry Pi 5, simplified. Every Pi has the same basic recipe: processor, RAM, storage on a microSD card, video out, USB, and the GPIO pins.
$15–120

Cheap enough to break

A Pi Zero 2 W is about $15; a top-end Pi 5 is about $120. Students can wire it wrong, reimage it, and try again.

LINUX

A real operating system

Raspberry Pi OS (Debian Linux) with a desktop, a browser, Python, Scratch, and a terminal. The same skills run real servers.

GPIO

Code meets the physical world

Forty pins turn led.on() into light, sound, and motion — the thing a Chromebook can't do.

Pi vs. Pico: a Raspberry Pi is a full computer that runs an operating system. A Raspberry Pi Pico is a $4–$7 microcontroller: no OS, it runs one program in MicroPython or C, and it's perfect for electronics. Same company, different jobs (see the table later).
Pass a bare Pi around the room while you talk. Ask: "What's missing compared to a laptop?" (screen, keyboard, battery, case — and the hard drive is a microSD card). Land the idea that it's a real computer, not a toy.

RetroPie + EmulationStation

RetroPie turns a Pi into a retro game console. It's free software built on Raspberry Pi OS that bundles two things: emulators that imitate old game hardware, and EmulationStation, the controller-friendly menu students actually see.

That stack is an AP CSP abstraction lesson hiding in plain sight: each layer hides the complexity of the one below it.

Why it works in a classroom

  • Boots straight into the game menu — no desktop to wander off into
  • Plays with cheap USB controllers
  • Can be locked in Kiosk mode so students can't change settings
  • Runs on a Pi 3, Pi 4, or Pi 400 out of the box

Know before you install

  • RetroPie's ready-made images target the Pi 4/400 and older. For a Pi 5, check retropie.org.uk for current support, or use Batocera or Recalbox, which offer Pi 5 builds.
  • RetroPie ships with no games. You supply them — legally (see Build It).
Show the menu on screen: scroll systems, open NES, show box art. Say the word "emulator" and ask the room to define it. Plant the seed: this layer diagram is a whole lesson on its own.

My Extra Credit Arcade

In my room, a Raspberry Pi running RetroPie is an arcade station students play for extra credit. Progress in a game turns into points in their Bank — our class extra credit account — and those points get spent on real grades. Students can also play the same library in a browser version of the arcade.

The loop

The points scale

22 classic NES and SNES games, sorted into four difficulty tiers. Points are earned per stage or level cleared, and they scale up the deeper into a game a student gets.

TierDifficultyPoints per levelExamples
1Easy5–20Entry-level games anyone can start on
2Medium10–30Most of the library
3Hard15–40For students who've beaten a Tier 2
4Brutal20–50Battletoads, Ninja Gaiden 3, Mega Man, Contra

The rules that make it work

Honest by design

  • Teacher verifies every level before points are logged — no self-reporting
  • Each game pays out once per school year, so nobody farms the easy levels
  • Save states and rewind stay off, so progress is real

Milestone bonuses for beating a whole game

  • A 100-point formative grade replacement, or
  • +10 points on a test, or
  • +2 on an exam (+3 for a Tier 4 game)
  • Capped at 3 milestone bonuses per semester

Where the points go: the Bank

Students check their balance

They sign in with their school Google account to see points earned, used, and available.

Students request to spend

They pick an assignment and ask to use points on it; I approve and apply it.

One bank, many sources

Arcade points sit alongside quiz bonuses and other extra credit, so it's one system, not five.

Why it works: retro games are hard in exactly the way learning is hard. You fail, you learn the pattern, you get one screen further. Students who never ask for extra credit will grind a Mega Man stage for twenty minutes, and that persistence is the point.
LIVE DEMO: have a volunteer play a Tier 1 game for about 60 seconds, then walk over and "verify" the level on screen exactly like you would in class. Then show the Bank with a demo student (not a real one). Hammer the three guardrails: teacher verifies, once per game per year, milestone cap.

Build Your Own Arcade

About $110–$130 and one planning period. Use a TV or projector you already have.

PartApprox. cost
Raspberry Pi 4 (4 GB)$55
Case with fan or heatsink$10–$15
Official USB-C power supply$8–$12
64 GB microSD card (A1/A2 rated)$10
2 USB SNES-style controllers$15–$25
Micro-HDMI to HDMI cable$8
Total≈ $110–$130

Keep it legal (and make it a lesson)

Downloading copies of commercial games you don't own is copyright infringement. Safe options:

  • Free homebrew games made by indie developers for old consoles
  • Games their rights holders have released for free
  • Games your students write (see Make It Yours)
  • Check your district's policy before loading anything

The "why is this illegal?" conversation is a ready-made digital citizenship lesson.

Setup, start to finish

  1. Flash the card. Install Raspberry Pi Imager on any computer, choose your Pi model, then Emulation and game OS → RetroPie, and write it to the microSD card.
  2. First boot. Plug in the controller, power on, and follow the prompts to map the buttons.
  3. Connect it. From the RetroPie menu, join Wi-Fi and turn on SSH. Ask your IT department first — school networks often need the device registered.
  4. Add games. Easiest way: on a USB stick, make a folder named retropie, plug it into the Pi until the light stops blinking, copy games into retropie-mount/roms/nes (or snes) on your computer, and plug it back in. Or copy over the network into ~/RetroPie/roms/.
  5. Scrape box art. Run the built-in scraper so every game shows cover art and a description.
  6. Lock it down. Set EmulationStation's UI Mode to Kiosk (no settings menu), hide systems you don't use, and disable RetroArch's save-state and rewind hotkeys so every level is earned.
  7. Back it up. Once it's perfect, copy the whole microSD card to an image file. If a card ever corrupts, you're back in business in ten minutes.
Classroom tips: use the official power supply (cheap ones cause random crashes and SD-card corruption); shut down from the menu instead of pulling the plug; label the controllers and keep them in a bin; and post a clear "when can you play" rule, such as only after the day's work is submitted.
If time allows, show the Imager menu on your laptop. Stress step 6: locking save states is what makes teacher verification meaningful. Remind everyone they'll have this page, so nobody needs to write anything down.

Make It Yours: Ways to Modify the Arcade

My version runs on a points bank and grade rewards. Here's how to bend it to fit your school, your grade level, and your comfort with gaming in class.

Change the reward

  • Non-grade rewards: homework pass, choose your seat, music during work time
  • Class-wide goal: every level anyone clears fills a thermometer toward a class celebration
  • Trade grade boosts for "retake a quiz" tickets

Change how progress is verified

  • Students photograph the stage-clear screen and submit it through a Google Form
  • Two-person rule: a classmate witnesses and initials a log sheet
  • A leaderboard spreadsheet students update after you initial it

Put learning in front of the game

  • Quiz to play: answer three review questions correctly to unlock a session
  • Bug bounty: find and fix a bug in a provided program to earn play time
  • Exit-ticket token: a strong exit ticket earns one arcade token

Change who makes the games

  • Students build Python/Pygame games that you add as a new "system" in EmulationStation; the best ones join the arcade
  • Middle schoolers publish Scratch games to a classroom Pi
  • Advanced students write a homebrew NES game in 6502 assembly

Middle school vs. high school

Middle schoolHigh school
GamesEasy-tier homebrew and student-made Scratch gamesThe full tier ladder, including brutal Tier 4
RewardPrivileges, class-wide goalsBank points toward grades, exam bonuses
Student rolePlayers and game testersBuilders: set up the Pi, write games, run the leaderboard
TimeScheduled "arcade Fridays"Anytime the day's work is done, with a sign-up sheet
Beyond extra credit: run a lunch or after-school tournament, use it as a club-recruiting magnet at open house, or build a bartop cabinet as a cross-curricular project with woodshop or engineering.
Ask the room: "Which of these would your admin approve tomorrow?" Take two or three answers. The takeaway: the system is the verify-then-reward loop, not the specific reward.

The CS Concepts Hiding in the Arcade

The arcade is a reward, but the machine itself is a curriculum. Every topic below comes straight from setting up, running, or explaining the RetroPie station. Open any card for a lesson you can run.

Emulation and abstractionAP CSP: Computing Systems & Networks

An emulator is software pretending to be hardware. The NES game only knows how to talk to a 6502 processor that isn't there, so the emulator imitates it, instruction by instruction.

Lesson: students draw the layer diagram (game → emulator → Linux → ARM chip) and explain what each layer hides from the one above it.

Processors and instruction setsHardware · Embedded Computing

The 1985 NES ran a 1.79 MHz 6502; a Pi 4 runs a quad-core ARM chip at 1.5 GHz or more — hundreds of times faster per core, which is why it can fake the old chip in real time.

Lesson: compare clock speed, cores, and RAM across a NES, a SNES, a Pi, and a student's phone. Why can't a NES emulate a Pi?

Binary, hex, and memoryAP CSP: Data

A ROM is just bytes. Cheat devices like the Game Genie worked by changing the value stored at a specific memory address.

Lesson: open a homebrew ROM in a hex viewer, find the header bytes (the first four spell NES plus an end-of-file byte), and convert them from hex to decimal and ASCII. Then discuss: an extra life is a single number in memory.

Operating systems and the Linux file systemIST: Operating Systems

Games live in ~/RetroPie/roms/nes; settings live in text files. Students see directories, paths, permissions, and why an operating system exists.

Lesson: a terminal scavenger hunt — use ls, cd, pwd, and df -h to find the games folder, count the games, and report free space.

NetworkingAP CSP: Computing Systems & Networks · IST: Networking

Adding games over the network means IP addresses, SSH, file transfer, and shared folders.

Lesson: find the Pi's IP address, ping it, SSH in, and copy a file across the network. Then trace what happened at each step.

Input devices and configurationHardware · Embedded Computing

A USB controller is just a device sending button codes; RetroPie maps each code to an action in a configuration file.

Lesson: remap the controller, find the file that changed, and read it. Extension: wire arcade buttons to the GPIO pins to build a custom controller.

Copyright, ethics, and intellectual propertyAP CSP: Impact of Computing · IST: Digital Citizenship

Why are emulators legal when most ROM downloads aren't? Who owns a 40-year-old game? What about games nobody sells anymore?

Lesson: a structured debate on game preservation vs. copyright, using real cases and the DMCA.

Algorithms and game designAP CSP: Algorithms & Programming

Enemy patterns are algorithms: loops, conditionals, and a little randomness. Beating a level means recognizing the pattern.

Lesson: students write pseudocode for one enemy's behavior, then build it in Python or Scratch.

Data collection and analysisAP CSP: Data

The points bank is a dataset: which games get played most, which tier is hardest, how fast students progress.

Lesson: give students anonymized arcade data to clean, chart, and draw conclusions from, then discuss what data they'd need to answer a better question.

CybersecurityIST · Cybersecurity pathways

An SSH-enabled Pi with a weak password is a real target on a real network.

Lesson: harden the arcade — strong password, updates, unused services turned off — and have students explain why each step matters.

Open two cards live — abstraction and copyright land best. Emphasize that every lesson here uses the machine they already bought for the arcade.

Starting an arcade?

Pi 4 (4 GB). The best RetroPie support and plenty of power for NES and SNES.

Starting a Pi lab?

Pi 500 or Pi 400. Keyboard, computer, and GPIO in one box — add a mouse and a monitor.

Starting electronics?

Pico W. At about $6, every student gets their own board to keep.

Add-ons worth knowing: the Sense HAT (sensors plus an LED matrix, the same board the Astro Pi program uses in space), the Camera Module, the Raspberry Pi AI Camera and AI Kit for on-device image recognition, and motor-driver boards for robots.

Getting Started + Q&A

You don't need a lab to start. One Pi, one TV, one idea.

WEEK 1

Buy one Pi 4 kit

Build the arcade (or any project from the list) on your own time first.

WEEK 2

Launch the loop

Pick your reward, your verification method, and your "when can you play" rule. Post them.

WEEK 3+

Teach the machine

Run one concept lesson from the arcade: abstraction, networking, or copyright.

Funding

  • DonorsChoose: Pi kits are classic, easy-to-fund projects
  • Local business and PTO mini-grants: "a $120 arcade that teaches CS" is an easy pitch
  • CTE / Perkins funds if your course is part of a career pathway
  • Start with one machine and add more as the budget allows
  • Ask your IT department about surplus monitors and keyboards
  • Old Pi 3s from past grants make perfect arcade machines
