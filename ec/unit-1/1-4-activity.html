Take a Stand: Who's Responsible for GPS Ankle Monitor Failures?

A wrongful arrest, a known GPS glitch, and three companies who each had a chance to stop it. No teams, no roles — just research, an opinion, and a room-wide debate.

00

The Scenario

A person on GPS ankle monitor supervision was arrested and jailed for 3 days after their monitor reported they'd left their approved zone. It turns out the monitor had a documented GPS drift error — a known glitch where the device occasionally reports a wildly wrong location for a few minutes at a time. The wrongly-jailed person is now demanding accountability.

This is a hypothetical case, built for today's discussion — none of the three companies below are real. Three could be at fault.
01

The Three Parties

Every part of this system is built and run by someone different — and each one had its own chance to catch the mistake.

Device Manufacturer

Meridian Trace Technologies

Designs and builds the actual ankle monitor hardware: the GPS chip, the strap, the battery, and the tamper sensor that detects if someone cuts it off.

How they could be at fault: internal testing had flagged the GPS drift glitch months before this arrest, but Meridian never issued a fix or a public warning about it — they kept shipping and running monitors they already knew could report a wrong location.
Monitoring Company

GuardLine Monitoring Services

Runs the 24/7 call center that receives the live GPS feed from every monitor in the program and decides which alerts to escalate to the supervising agency.

How they could be at fault: GuardLine's own protocol requires a second, confirming GPS ping before escalating a zone-violation alert — but the on-duty operator escalated after a single drifted reading, skipping the confirmation step to clear a backlog of alerts that night.
Supervision Agency

Millbrook County Office of Community Supervision

The government agency that required this person to wear the monitor as a condition of release, with legal authority to act on an alert and send someone back to jail.

How they could be at fault: county policy requires an officer to attempt a phone call to the supervised person before requesting a warrant off a monitor alert alone — the on-call officer skipped that call, citing a new "zero-tolerance" directive her supervisor had issued just that week.
02

How It Works

No advance setup required — just today's research and a room to move around in.

  1. After today's AI research, Mr. Norman posts three spots in the room: Meridian Trace Technologies (device manufacturer), GuardLine Monitoring Services (monitoring company), Millbrook County Office of Community Supervision (supervision agency).
  2. Everyone physically moves to stand by whichever one they think is most responsible for the wrongful arrest.
  3. Mr. Norman calls on a few students from each group to explain their reasoning out loud — you have to use a specific fact from today's research, not just an opinion.
  4. After hearing the other groups' arguments, you can switch spots if you're genuinely convinced — that's allowed and encouraged, not a sign you were wrong to start.
  5. We close with everyone writing a short individual reflection.
Nothing to prep beyond today's AI research — no teams to assign, no roles, no script. If you end up standing alone or with just one or two others at a spot, that's fine — you still have to defend it out loud. You can cite real precedent if it helps your case — the 2015 Fitbit murder case (wearable data used as courtroom evidence) and documented GPS monitor drift errors in real domestic-violence protective-order cases are both real, citable examples.

Not another forensic report today — just research, an opinion, and a room-wide debate.

Pick a side, defend it with a fact, and be honest if someone else's argument actually changes your mind.
