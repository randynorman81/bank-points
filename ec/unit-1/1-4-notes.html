GPS, Data & the Law: Who's Watching, and Who Answers For It

A GPS ankle monitor is really three systems stacked on top of each other — a positioning system, a transmission link, and a monitoring layer — and behind all three sits a legal question about who pays when one of them fails.

00

GPS

GPS (Global Positioning System) is a network of satellites that lets a small receiver calculate its own location on Earth — no cell signal or internet required just to figure out where it is.

In orbit

The satellites

Dozens of satellites constantly broadcast a signal containing their exact position and the exact time it was sent. A receiver needs signals from at least four of them to calculate its own location through a process called trilateration.

On the ground

The receiver

The chip inside the ankle monitor listens for those signals and measures how long each one took to arrive. Tiny timing differences between satellites are what let it calculate a precise latitude and longitude.

GPS isn't perfect. Buildings, weather, and hardware bugs can all cause GPS drift — a known error where a device briefly reports a location that's wrong, sometimes by a significant distance, before correcting itself. That single flaw is at the center of today's case.
01

Data Transmission

Data transmission is how a calculated location actually leaves the device and reaches a human who can act on it. GPS tells the monitor where it is; transmission is the separate job of reporting that somewhere else, usually over a cellular network.

Alert type 01

Zone-violation alert

Sent the moment the calculated GPS position falls outside an approved boundary — the alert a monitoring service treats as most urgent.

Alert type 02

Tamper alert

Sent when a sensor detects the strap has been cut, stretched, or removed, independent of whatever the GPS chip is reporting.

Alert type 03

Battery-low alert

A routine status update, not an emergency — but it still has to travel the same transmission path as the urgent alerts.

Transmission takes time and isn't guaranteed to be instant or perfectly reliable. A weak signal can delay an alert, and a delayed alert can make a location that's already corrected itself look like an active, ongoing violation.
02

Monitoring System

A monitoring system is the combination of software and staff that receives every transmitted alert, decides which ones are real, and escalates the real ones to whoever has authority to act.

Automated layer

The software

Watches the incoming stream of alerts around the clock and flags anything that crosses a threshold — instantly, and without judgment about whether the reading actually makes sense.

Human layer

The operator

Reviews a flagged alert against a written protocol before escalating it further — the step where a human is supposed to catch a false alarm the software can't recognize on its own.

A monitoring system is only as reliable as the protocol its operators actually follow. A perfectly good rule — like requiring a second confirming reading before escalating — does nothing if it gets skipped under pressure.
03

Civil Liability

Civil liability is legal responsibility for causing harm or loss to another person, which can require paying monetary damages. It's a separate track from criminal liability, and a single failure can trigger either one, both, or neither.

QuestionCivil liabilityCriminal liability
Who brings the case?The harmed person (a lawsuit)The government (a prosecution)
What's the goal?Compensate the person who was harmedPunish a violation of the law
Possible outcomeOrdered to pay damagesFines and/or jail time
Standard of proofMore likely than notBeyond a reasonable doubt
This is exactly why today's activity has three possible parties instead of one obvious villain: a device manufacturer, a monitoring company, and a government agency can each be civilly liable in a different way, for a different specific failure, in the very same incident.

Three technical systems, stacked on top of each other, and a legal question sitting on top of all three.

GPS calculates a location, transmission carries it somewhere, a monitoring system decides what to do about it — and civil liability is how the law sorts out who answers for it when any layer gets it wrong.
