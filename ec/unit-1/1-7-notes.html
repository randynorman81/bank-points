1.7 Notes: AI in Embedded Computing and IoT

Five terms, one real design question underneath all of them: when a device "thinks," where does that thinking actually happen?

Jump to What Counts as AI? Edge vs. Cloud Embedded AI Putting It Together

What Counts as AI?

Artificial Intelligence

A broad field — any computer system built to do something that normally requires human-like judgment: recognizing a face, understanding speech, predicting what you'll type next. It's a category, not one specific technique.

Machine Learning

The technique behind almost all modern AI. Instead of a human writing an explicit rule for every situation, the system is shown a large number of examples and learns the pattern itself — pattern-finding from data, instead of hand-written rules.

Edge AI vs. Cloud AI

Once you've got a trained model, it has to run somewhere. That's the real design decision for anyone building an AI-powered embedded device — and it's a tradeoff, not a clear winner.

Edge AICloud AI
Where it thinksDirectly on the device itselfOn a remote server, over the internet
SpeedFast — no round trip over a networkSlower — data has to travel there and back
PrivacyStronger — nothing has to leave the deviceWeaker — your data is sent somewhere else
Processing powerLimited by the device's tiny chip and batteryEffectively unlimited — real data centers
Works offline?YesNo — needs an internet connection
ExampleA smart camera recognizing a face on-deviceA voice assistant sending your question to a server for a full answer
Most real products aren't purely one or the other — a lot of devices do something simple and fast on the edge (like noticing a face is present at all) and hand off anything that needs real reasoning to the cloud (like figuring out whose face it is, or answering a spoken question).

5 Examples

Edge AI

1

Phone face/fingerprint unlock

Your face scan is matched against your stored face data right on the phone's chip. It has to be instant, and your biometric data staying on the device instead of traveling to a server is a real security requirement, not a nice-to-have.

2

Smart doorbell person-detection

Before it ever sends you a notification, the camera has to decide locally: is this a person, a car, or just a tree branch moving in the wind? Filtering on-device saves your data plan and the company's server costs.

3

Self-driving car object detection

Recognizing a pedestrian, a stop sign, or a car braking ahead has to happen in milliseconds. A cloud round-trip is too slow at highway speed, and the car can't stop working the moment it loses signal.

4

Voice assistant wake-word detection

The always-listening part that catches "Hey Siri" or "Alexa" runs locally on purpose — streaming every sound in your house to a server just to catch its name would be a privacy problem and a battery drain.

5

Factory equipment vibration monitoring

A sensor on a motor or bearing has to notice an abnormal vibration the instant it starts, so the machine can shut down before it breaks. Factory floors don't always have reliable internet either.

5 Examples

Cloud AI

1

Voice assistants answering open questions

Once you ask something that actually requires reasoning ("what's a good gift for a hiker?"), the request goes to a server running a massive language model — far too large to ever fit on a speaker or phone.

2

Streaming recommendation engines

Netflix or Spotify suggesting what you'll like next works by comparing your habits against millions of other users at once. No single device could store or process a dataset that size.

3

Photo library content search

Searching your entire photo library for "dog at the beach" means scanning potentially years of photos — far more storage and processing than a phone can do continuously.

4

Large-scale email spam filtering

Catching a new phishing scam depends on recognizing patterns seen across millions of inboxes at once, and that shared threat model has to update constantly. Only a central server sees enough traffic.

5

AI-assisted medical image analysis

Software that helps a radiologist spot something on an X-ray or CT scan runs on an extremely large, carefully validated model that needs continual retraining and serious computing power.

Embedded AI

Embedded AI is what you get when a trained Machine Learning model is shrunk down to actually fit and run on a small, dedicated embedded system — the exact kind of device you defined back in Lesson 1.1: a computer system built into a larger device to do one specific job, not a general-purpose computer. Embedded AI is just that same idea, with "thinking" as the specific job.

Embedded AI overlaps with edge AI — both mean "thinking happens locally." The difference is what kind of device it's running on: edge AI can run on a phone or a laptop, both general-purpose computers with plenty of power to spare. Embedded AI specifically means a tiny, dedicated, single-purpose chip, usually with real power and real-time constraints a phone never has to worry about.

5 Examples

Embedded AI

1

Learning thermostats

A small dedicated chip in the wall learns your household's temperature patterns and adjusts automatically. It has to keep controlling your heat and air even if your Wi-Fi goes down.

2

Fall detection on a smartwatch

Recognizing a hard fall has to happen instantly, on the tiny low-power chip inside the watch itself, because the moment it matters most is exactly when a cloud connection can't be counted on.

3

Pacemaker rhythm monitoring

An AI-assisted pacemaker has to notice an irregular heartbeat and react within a fraction of a second, inside the body — with no acceptable version that waits on a network request.

4

Robot vacuum obstacle recognition

Telling a wall apart from a pet or a person has to happen continuously and cheaply on a small onboard chip. A full general-purpose computer would drain its small battery in minutes.

5

Hearing aid noise cancellation

Cleaning up background noise from speech has to happen with essentially zero delay. That only works on a dedicated chip small enough to fit in the ear, running for days on a tiny battery.

Putting It Together

When you're investigating a real AI-powered product for today's assignment, ask yourself, in order:

  1. What is this device actually deciding or recognizing? (That's the AI part.)
  2. Does it need an internet connection to do that, or does it still work offline? (That tells you Edge vs. Cloud.)
  3. What's the tradeoff the company made, and why might they have made it that way — speed and privacy, or raw power?
