1.5 — Mock Trial: Who's Responsible for GPS Ankle Monitor Failures?

Not another forensic report today — you already wrote one for Therac-25 and one for Toyota. Today you argue the case out loud instead.

Intro

Ask AI these questions; look over the answers.

  1. How do GPS ankle monitors work and what are they used for in the criminal justice system?
  2. What are some documented failures of GPS ankle monitors? Give me one specific real example of a monitor malfunctioning.
  3. Explain to a middle schooler how a GPS sensor in an ankle monitor knows where a person is located.
  4. Create a simple diagram showing the path data takes from a GPS ankle monitor to an alert reaching a monitoring center.
  5. Who profits from GPS ankle monitor technology, and how does the criminal justice system pay for it? Does this raise any ethical concerns?
  6. If a GPS ankle monitor falsely reports a location and someone is sent back to jail because of it, who should be held responsible — the device manufacturer, the monitoring company, or the government agency that required the device?

Today's Activity — Mock Trial

📄 Trial Script & Run of Show — the full minute-by-minute script for whoever runs the trial, with every line to say and every timing cue already written out.

The scenario: a person on GPS ankle monitor supervision was arrested and jailed for 3 days after their monitor reported they'd left their approved zone. It turns out the monitor had a documented GPS drift error — a known glitch where the device occasionally reports a wildly wrong location for a few minutes at a time. The wrongly-jailed person is now demanding accountability. Three parties could be at fault: the device manufacturer (built a monitor with a known flaw), the monitoring company (didn't flag the reading as suspicious before alerting authorities), and the supervision agency (arrested someone off a single automated alert with no human check).

Roles and team sizes — sized for a class of about 22 (11 pairs). If your actual class is a different size, keep all 4 teams the same size as each other and put everyone left over on the jury — the jury should always be the biggest group in the room, never smaller than a single team.

Exactly what each role does — read this before prep time starts:

Judge (1 student, or Mr. Norman if you'd rather keep tighter control of pacing):

  • Keeps the clock. Calls each team up in this fixed order: Plaintiff, then Defense/Manufacturer, then Defense/Monitoring Company, then Defense/Supervision Agency.
  • After all four opening arguments, opens the floor for jury cross-examination and calls on one juror at a time.
  • Has final say if two sides disagree about whether something is a real fact from today's research or a made-up claim.
  • Announces the switch to jury deliberation at 1:05 and to reading verdicts at 1:20.
  • Does not argue for any side and does not vote on the verdict — stays neutral the entire period.

Plaintiff's team (3 students) — represents the wrongly-jailed person:

  • Pick ONE defendant your team believes is most responsible. You can mention the other two were also careless, but your argument has to commit to one primary target — don't blame all three equally.
  • Before prep time ends, split these 3 jobs among your team: (1) delivers the 3-minute opening argument, (2) keeps a written list of the specific facts from today's AI research your team is using as evidence, (3) answers jury cross-examination questions during the trial.
  • Turn in: your team's opening argument, written out, citing at least 2 specific facts from today's research. One submission for the whole team.

Defense teams (3 students each) — Device Manufacturer, Monitoring Company, Supervision Agency:

  • You are only defending the ONE defendant your team was assigned — don't try to defend all three.
  • Your goal is to convince the jury your defendant is NOT the one at fault. You're allowed to argue one of the other two defendants is actually responsible instead — but you have to explain specifically why, using how the technology or the process actually works, not just "it's their fault."
  • Same 3 internal jobs as the plaintiff's team: one delivers the opening argument, one tracks your evidence, one handles cross-examination.
  • Turn in: your team's opening argument, written out, citing at least 2 specific facts from today's research. One submission for the whole team.

Jury (everyone not on a team or the judge, about 9 students):

  • During all four opening arguments: take individual notes. You need these for your verdict later — you can't rely on memory alone.
  • When the judge opens the floor for cross-examination, be ready to ask at least one real clarifying question to at least one team if you're called on.
  • During deliberation you can talk quietly with jurors near you, but your verdict is your own — this is not a group vote. Every juror writes and turns in their own individual verdict.
  • Turn in: your own written verdict, 3–4 sentences, naming the ONE defendant you found most responsible and citing at least one specific fact from the trial that convinced you.

Today's schedule:

  • 0:00–0:15 — AI research (the questions above) — this is your evidence gathering, before roles are assigned
  • 0:15–0:35 — Roles assigned; team prep — build your argument and split your team's 3 internal jobs
  • 0:35–1:05 — Trial: the judge calls each team up in order for a 3-minute opening argument, then opens the floor for jury cross-examination
  • 1:05–1:20 — Jury deliberates quietly; every juror writes their own individual verdict
  • 1:20–1:30 — A few jurors read their verdicts aloud; Developer Log

Rules: every argument needs at least one real fact from today's AI research — no made-up claims; the judge has final say on any dispute about this. You can point blame at the other defendants, but you have to explain specifically why, using how the technology actually works. Jurors: your verdict needs a reason, not just a name. You can cite real precedent if it helps your case — the 2015 Fitbit murder case (wearable data used as courtroom evidence) and documented GPS monitor drift errors in real domestic-violence protective-order cases are both real, citable examples.

Assignments

  • Plaintiff's team and all 3 defense teams: submit your team's opening argument in writing (½–1 page), citing at least 2 specific facts from today's research. One submission per team, not one per student.
  • Jurors: submit your own individual written verdict, 3–4 sentences, naming which defendant you found most responsible and why.
  • Judge: no written submission today — your job was running the room. Use your developer log entry to reflect on what it took to keep the trial fair and on time.
  • Fill out your developer report on your Google Site every class period (graded at the end of the unit; timestamps checked) — reflect specifically on the trial, not just the case facts.
