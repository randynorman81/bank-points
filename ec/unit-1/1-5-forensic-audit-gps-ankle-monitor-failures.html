1.5 — Mock Trial: Who's Responsible for GPS Ankle Monitor Failures?

Not another forensic report today — you already wrote one for Therac-25 and one for Toyota. Today you argue the case out loud instead.

Intro

Ask AI these questions; look over the answers.

  1. How do GPS ankle monitors work and what are they used for in the criminal justice system?
  2. What are some documented failures of GPS ankle monitors? Give me one specific real example of a monitor malfunctioning.
  3. Explain to a middle schooler how a GPS sensor in an ankle monitor knows where a person is located.
  4. Create a simple diagram showing the path data takes from a GPS ankle monitor to an alert reaching a monitoring center.
  5. Who profits from GPS ankle monitor technology, and how does the criminal justice system pay for it? Does this raise any ethical concerns?
  6. If a GPS ankle monitor falsely reports a location and someone is sent back to jail because of it, who should be held responsible — the device manufacturer, the monitoring company, or the government agency that required the device?

Today's Activity — Mock Trial

The scenario: a person on GPS ankle monitor supervision was arrested and jailed for 3 days after their monitor reported they'd left their approved zone. It turns out the monitor had a documented GPS drift error — a known glitch where the device occasionally reports a wildly wrong location for a few minutes at a time. The wrongly-jailed person is now demanding accountability. Three parties could be at fault: the device manufacturer (built a monitor with a known flaw), the monitoring company (didn't flag the reading as suspicious before alerting authorities), and the supervision agency (arrested someone off a single automated alert with no human check).

Roles (assigned in class):

  • Plaintiff's team — represents the wrongly-jailed person; argues someone owes them accountability
  • Defense teams (one per defendant) — Device Manufacturer, Monitoring Company, Supervision Agency; each argues they aren't the responsible party
  • Jury — everyone not on a trial team; takes notes, asks each side one clarifying question, delivers a written verdict at the end

Today's schedule:

  • 0:00–0:15 — AI research (the questions above) — this is your evidence gathering
  • 0:15–0:35 — Team prep: build your argument using real facts from your research
  • 0:35–1:05 — Trial: each team gives a 3-minute opening argument, then answers cross-examination questions from the jury
  • 1:05–1:20 — Jury deliberates; each juror writes their verdict
  • 1:20–1:30 — A few jurors read their verdicts aloud; Developer Log

Rules: every argument needs at least one real fact from today's AI research — no made-up claims. You can point blame at the other defendants, but you have to explain specifically why, using how the technology actually works. Jurors: your verdict needs a reason, not just a name. You can cite real precedent if it helps your case — the 2015 Fitbit murder case (wearable data used as courtroom evidence) and documented GPS monitor drift errors in real domestic-violence protective-order cases are both real, citable examples.

Assignments

  • Trial teams: submit your team's opening argument in writing (½–1 page), citing at least 2 specific facts from today's research.
  • Jurors: submit your written verdict with 3–4 sentences of reasoning, naming which party you found most responsible and why.
  • Fill out your developer report on your Google Site every class period (graded at the end of the unit; timestamps checked) — reflect specifically on the trial, not just the case facts.
