1.5 — Take a Stand: Who's Responsible for GPS Ankle Monitor Failures?

Not another forensic report today — you already wrote one for Therac-25 and one for Toyota. No teams, no roles, no prep to set up. Just research, an opinion, and a room-wide debate.

Intro

Ask AI these questions; look over the answers.

  1. How do GPS ankle monitors work and what are they used for in the criminal justice system?
  2. What are some documented failures of GPS ankle monitors? Give me one specific real example of a monitor malfunctioning.
  3. Explain to a middle schooler how a GPS sensor in an ankle monitor knows where a person is located.
  4. Create a simple diagram showing the path data takes from a GPS ankle monitor to an alert reaching a monitoring center.
  5. Who profits from GPS ankle monitor technology, and how does the criminal justice system pay for it? Does this raise any ethical concerns?
  6. If a GPS ankle monitor falsely reports a location and someone is sent back to jail because of it, who should be held responsible — the device manufacturer, the monitoring company, or the government agency that required the device?

Today's Activity — Take a Stand

The scenario: a person on GPS ankle monitor supervision was arrested and jailed for 3 days after their monitor reported they'd left their approved zone. It turns out the monitor had a documented GPS drift error — a known glitch where the device occasionally reports a wildly wrong location for a few minutes at a time. The wrongly-jailed person is now demanding accountability. This is a hypothetical case, built for today's discussion — none of the three companies below are real. Three could be at fault:

  • Meridian Trace Technologies (Device Manufacturer) — designs and builds the actual ankle monitor hardware: the GPS chip, the strap, the battery, and the tamper sensor that detects if someone cuts it off. How they could be at fault: internal testing had flagged the GPS drift glitch months before this arrest, but Meridian never issued a fix or a public warning about it — they kept shipping and running monitors they already knew could report a wrong location.
  • GuardLine Monitoring Services (Monitoring Company) — runs the 24/7 call center that receives the live GPS feed from every monitor in the program. GuardLine doesn't build any hardware; its job is to watch incoming zone-violation and tamper alerts, judge which ones look real, and decide which to escalate to the supervising agency. How they could be at fault: GuardLine's own protocol requires a second, confirming GPS ping before escalating a zone-violation alert — but the on-duty operator escalated after a single drifted reading, skipping the confirmation step to clear a backlog of alerts that night.
  • Millbrook County Office of Community Supervision (Supervision Agency) — the government agency that required this person to wear the monitor as a condition of release. Millbrook County contracts with GuardLine to do the actual monitoring, and its officers are the ones with legal authority to act on an alert and send someone back to jail. How they could be at fault: county policy requires an officer to attempt a phone call to the supervised person before requesting a warrant off a monitor alert alone — the on-call officer skipped that call, citing a new "zero-tolerance" directive her supervisor had issued just that week.

How it works — no advance setup required:

  • After today's AI research, Mr. Norman posts three spots in the room: Meridian Trace Technologies (device manufacturer), GuardLine Monitoring Services (monitoring company), Millbrook County Office of Community Supervision (supervision agency).
  • Everyone physically moves to stand by whichever one they think is most responsible for the wrongful arrest.
  • Mr. Norman calls on a few students from each group to explain their reasoning out loud — you have to use a specific fact from today's research, not just an opinion.
  • After hearing the other groups' arguments, you can switch spots if you're genuinely convinced — that's allowed and encouraged, not a sign you were wrong to start.
  • We close with everyone writing a short individual reflection.

Nothing to prep beyond today's AI research — no teams to assign, no roles, no script. If you end up standing alone or with just one or two others at a spot, that's fine — you still have to defend it out loud. You can cite real precedent if it helps your case — the 2015 Fitbit murder case (wearable data used as courtroom evidence) and documented GPS monitor drift errors in real domestic-violence protective-order cases are both real, citable examples.
