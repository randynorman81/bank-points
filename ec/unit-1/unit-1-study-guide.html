1.1 — What Is an Embedded System?

Embedded system
A computer system built into a larger device to perform one specific, dedicated task — not a flexible, general-purpose computer. The control board inside a microwave oven is a good example: it will run its one program for the rest of its existence and nothing else.
The real distinguishing question
It's not about size or power — it's about whether the device was designed to run one fixed job decided at design time, or whether a user can install new, arbitrary software on it later. You can't turn a microwave's control board into a word processor. You can turn a laptop into almost anything.
Microcontroller vs. microprocessor
A microcontroller packs the CPU, memory, and I/O onto a single chip — everything the one job needs, all in one place. Used in embedded systems. A microprocessor (like the one in a laptop) is just the CPU core; it needs separate memory and peripheral chips wired around it externally to do anything.
Why the distinction matters
An embedded system trades flexibility for reliability, low cost, low power use, and predictable, real-time behavior. That tradeoff is exactly why a pacemaker or a car's brake controller is built as a dedicated embedded system instead of a general-purpose computer — you want it to behave identically every single time, with no risk of a user-installed app introducing a bug.
Examples across categories
Household (microwave, washing machine, thermostat), medical (pacemaker, insulin pump), automotive (engine control unit, anti-lock brakes), industrial (factory floor sensors). Notice these span almost every part of daily life — embedded systems vastly outnumber general-purpose computers in the real world.

1.2 — Forensic Auditing & the Therac-25

Purpose of a forensic audit
To answer two separate questions: what technically went wrong, and who is responsible for it. These aren't the same question — a technical root cause and legal/professional accountability can point in different directions.
The Therac-25
A computer-controlled radiation therapy machine from the mid-1980s that could deliver treatment two ways: a low-power electron beam applied directly, or a high-power X-ray beam that required a physical beam-spreading attachment to be moved into place first, since the concentrated high-power beam was only safe once spread out.
What actually went wrong
Between 1985 and 1987, at least six documented incidents delivered massive radiation overdoses — up to roughly 100 times the intended dose — causing severe radiation burns and multiple deaths. The cause was a race condition: if an experienced operator typed quickly enough to correct a setup mistake within about 8 seconds, the software's check for "is the beam-spreader correctly in position" could be skipped, letting the unspread, high-power beam fire directly at the patient.
Race condition
A bug where the outcome depends on the relative timing or order of two things happening, because that timing isn't properly controlled. In the Therac-25's case: the software's safety check and the operator's typing speed were racing each other, and typing fast enough won.
The removed safety feature
Earlier machines (the Therac-6 and Therac-20) had hardware safety interlocks — physical, mechanical safeguards that made the dangerous state impossible regardless of what the software did. The Therac-25 removed those hardware interlocks and relied entirely on software to catch the same class of error, turning one single point of failure into a life-or-death risk.
Why this case anchors the whole course
It's the origin case for a theme you'll see again later this year: software alone is often not considered sufficient protection for a safety-critical system. A second, independent, physical layer of protection catches what software might miss — and removing that layer to save cost or complexity is exactly how a single bug becomes a disaster.

1.3 — Embedded Systems All Around Us

Arduino
An umbrella term for a board design, its software (the Arduino IDE), and the community around it — not the same thing as the microcontroller chip itself, which is the actual component doing the computing that happens to sit on the board.
Raspberry Pi vs. Arduino
A Raspberry Pi is a small full computer that runs a complete operating system and can multitask — closer to a general-purpose computer. An Arduino is a simpler microcontroller board built to run exactly one program, continuously, with no operating system in the traditional sense — closer to the strict definition of an embedded system.
IoT
Stands for Internet of Things — physical, embedded devices connected to a network so they can send or receive data, and often be monitored or controlled remotely.
Pacemaker
An example of an embedded system where a software failure could be life-threatening, since it directly and continuously controls a person's heart rhythm. Same underlying stakes as the Therac-25, just continuous and preventive rather than a single treatment event.

1.5 — Take a Stand: GPS Ankle Monitor Failures

GPS ankle monitor
Tracks a supervised person's real-time location and sends zone-violation or tamper alerts to a monitoring service, which can escalate to the supervising government agency.
The three parties and their specific failure points
Meridian Trace Technologies (device manufacturer) — kept shipping devices with a GPS drift bug it had already been warned about. GuardLine Monitoring Services (monitoring company) — skipped its own required second-confirmation-ping step before escalating an alert. Millbrook County Office of Community Supervision (government agency) — skipped a required verification phone call before acting on the alert, due to a new zero-tolerance policy.
Why three parties, not one
The point of the scenario: a real failure is rarely caused by one bad actor. It's usually a chain of smaller failures across the people who build a system, the people who operate it, and the people with authority to act on it — a pattern that reappears in every later case study this course.
Real-world precedent cited
The 2015 Fitbit murder case — wearable device step and heart-rate data was used as courtroom evidence, helping to disprove a suspect's account of events. Establishes that personal device data can carry real legal weight, not just personal curiosity.
Civil liability
Legal responsibility for causing harm or loss to another person, which can require paying monetary damages — distinct from criminal liability, which involves government prosecution and potential jail time.

1.7 — AI in Embedded Computing and IoT

Artificial Intelligence vs. Machine Learning
Artificial Intelligence is the broad category: any system built to do something that normally requires human-like judgment. Machine Learning is the specific technique behind almost all modern AI — instead of a programmer writing an explicit rule for every situation, the system is shown many examples and learns the pattern itself.
Edge AI vs. Cloud AI
Edge AI processes data directly on the device, which is faster and more private since nothing leaves the device, but limited by the device's small chip and battery. Cloud AI sends data to a remote server for processing, which is far more powerful but slower and requires an internet connection. Most real products use some mix of both.
Embedded AI
What you get when a trained Machine Learning model is shrunk down to run on a small, dedicated embedded system — the same kind of device defined in Lesson 1.1. It overlaps with edge AI, but edge AI can run on any local device (a phone counts); embedded AI specifically means a tiny, single-purpose chip.
Why the location of "thinking" is a real design decision
A self-driving car's object detection has to run on the edge — a cloud round-trip is too slow at highway speed, and the car can't stop working the moment it loses signal. A voice assistant answering an open-ended question needs the cloud — the language model required is far too large to fit on a speaker. Neither choice is automatically "better"; it depends on what the device actually needs.
