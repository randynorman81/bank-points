1.7 — AI in Embedded Computing and IoT

Extra Credit Quiz

Today we'll take the 1.7 Quiz — 10 questions for extra credit. The first 5 cover earlier material; the last 5 cover this unit.

Notes

Look up these definitions:

  • Artificial Intelligence
  • Edge AI
  • Cloud AI
  • Machine Learning
  • Embedded AI

Key idea: Artificial Intelligence is a broad field — any computer system built to do something that normally takes human-like judgment, like recognizing a face, understanding speech, or predicting what you'll type next. Machine Learning is the technique behind most modern AI: instead of a programmer writing an explicit rule for every possible situation, the system is shown a large number of examples and learns the pattern itself. The real design question for an embedded device is where that thinking actually happens. Edge AI means the processing runs directly on the device — a smart camera recognizing a face without ever sending the image anywhere. Cloud AI means the device sends its data over the internet to a powerful remote server, which does the thinking and sends an answer back. Edge AI is usually faster and more private, since nothing leaves the device; cloud AI can be far more powerful, since it isn't limited by a tiny chip's memory or battery — most real products actually use some mix of both. Embedded AI is what you get when a trained Machine Learning model gets shrunk down to fit onto a small, dedicated embedded system — the exact kind of device you've been defining since Lesson 1.1. Today's assignment asks you to find a real one and figure out how it actually thinks.

Assignments

1.7 — AI Product Investigation Team. Real companies researching a competitor's product almost never send one person to figure it out alone — they send a small team, each person covering a different angle, then combine what they found. You're doing the same thing today in a 3-person Investigation Team. Each role has a specific, checkable job.

This is a shared team grade. If your team's submission is missing any one role's required piece, the whole team's grade drops 15 points — not just that person's share. Choose teammates who will actually do their part, and hold each other to it.

Role 1 — Technical Lead
Figures out how the product's AI actually works. Is it Edge AI, Cloud AI, or a hybrid of both? What embedded hardware or sensors is it probably using? Where does Machine Learning fit in? Writes the Technical Analysis section below.
Role 2 — Impact Analyst
Figures out the real-world side. What specific problem does this product solve, who actually uses it, and what real controversies, failures, or privacy concerns has it faced, if any? Writes the Real-World Impact section below.
Role 3 — Case Presenter
Combines Roles 1 and 2's research into the Takeaway section below, assembles the final Doc or Slides, and presents it to the class in under 2 minutes. Every team member should still be ready to answer a question about their own role's research if called on.

What to Turn In

One shared file per team — a Google Doc or a Google Slides deck, your team's choice. Create it, share editing access with your two teammates so all three of you can build it together, and put it together in this exact order:

  1. Title. The product's name, and all three team members' names with their role (Technical Lead / Impact Analyst / Case Presenter).
  2. Technical Analysis (Technical Lead). Is this product Edge AI, Cloud AI, or a hybrid — and how do you know? Explain your reasoning. Include at least one specific, real fact about the actual hardware or sensors involved (not a guess).
  3. Real-World Impact (Impact Analyst). What specific problem does this product solve, and who actually uses it? Include at least one specific real incident, review, or news story — name the source (a link, an article title, or a reviewer). "People say it's good/bad" is not a source.
  4. Takeaway (Case Presenter, written after reading Roles 1 and 2's sections). 2–3 sentences connecting the technical side to the real-world impact — why did this product's designers make the Edge/Cloud choice they made, given what it's actually used for?
  5. Sources. Links or citations for every specific fact and incident above.

Doc: keep it to roughly one page, with each section above clearly labeled with a heading. Slides: one slide per section above (5 slides total) works well and doubles as your presentation deck.

How to Submit

One team member submits the shared Doc or Slides link to Google Classroom under today's 1.7 assignment, before the end of the period. Double-check your sharing settings allow Mr. Norman to open it. Only one submission per team is needed — but because all three names are on the file itself, everyone gets graded on it, and everyone is on the hook for the 15-point team penalty if a section is missing or clearly unfinished.

Pick any AI-powered embedded product your team can dig into with real sources — a voice assistant, a robot vacuum, a fitness tracker, a self-driving feature, an AI security camera, a delivery drone, a smart traffic system, a robotic lawn mower, an AI hearing aid, a pet-monitoring camera.
