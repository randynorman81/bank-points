12.3 — Arithmetic, Comparison, and Logical Operators (Ultrasonic Sensor)

🔔 Bell Ringer — Think About It
  1. An ultrasonic range finder measures distance by timing a sound pulse bouncing back. Why might a sensor like this be useful in a real product (like a parking sensor or a robot)?
  2. This lesson combines arithmetic, comparison, and logical operators — all things you practiced in Conditionals and Looping. What do you think a program would need to do with a raw distance reading before it's useful?
  3. If distance < 10, what real-world meaning might that condition represent for a robot or alarm system?
  4. Why do sensors usually need some math and logic applied to their raw reading, instead of the raw number being directly useful on its own?

Notes

Look up these definitions:

  • Ultrasonic range finder
  • Arithmetic operators (review)
  • Comparison / logical operators (review)

Key idea: An ultrasonic range finder measures distance by timing how long a sound pulse takes to bounce off something and return, and this lesson has you write programs that use arithmetic, comparison, and logical operators together to turn that raw reading into a decision — for example, if distance < 10, trigger an alert. This is the payoff of the whole Conditionals module: a sensor's raw number is rarely useful by itself, it needs operators and logic applied to become a decision a program can act on.
