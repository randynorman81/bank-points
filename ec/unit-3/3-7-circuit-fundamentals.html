3.7 — Circuit Fundamentals: Measuring Ohm's Law for Real

🔔 Bell Ringer — Think About It
  1. You calculated a predicted resistor value and current in 2.7 using pure math. Today you'll measure the real thing with a multimeter. If your measured current is a little different from your calculated prediction, does that mean your math was wrong?
  2. A pull-up resistor's whole purpose is preventing a "floating" pin. On a real breadboard, could you actually observe a floating pin behaving unpredictably, the way the lesson describes? How would you even test that?
  3. Percent error is a formula for comparing a measured value to a predicted one. Why is a small percent error something to expect and accept, while a huge percent error should make you suspect a wiring mistake instead of "real-world imprecision"?
  4. Ohm's Law told you exactly what resistor to use before you ever touched a component. Now that you can verify it with a multimeter, has today made you trust the formula more, or does measuring it yourself feel different from trusting the math alone?

Notes

Look up these definitions:

  • Source current
  • Sink current
  • Pull-up resistor
  • Floating pin
  • Percent error

Key idea: wire the same INPUT_PULLUP button circuit and LED-with-resistor circuit from 2.7, for real. Use a multimeter to measure the actual voltage drop across your resistor and your LED, and the actual current flowing through the circuit. Calculate percent error — |measured − predicted| / predicted × 100 — between your 2.7 calculated current and today's measured current. A few percent is normal (component tolerance, multimeter accuracy); a huge gap usually means a wiring mistake, not a flaw in Ohm's Law itself.
