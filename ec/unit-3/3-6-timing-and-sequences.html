3.6 — Timing and Sequences (Real Hardware)

🔔 Bell Ringer — Think About It
  1. Your three-LED sequence from 2.6 ran on a fixed loop, no matter what. Today you're adding a real pushbutton that can interrupt that pattern on demand. Why is "interrupting a running program" a fundamentally harder problem than just running a longer fixed sequence?
  2. A real pedestrian crossing button doesn't change the light immediately — it waits until a safe point in the cycle. Why do you think real traffic engineers designed it that way instead of switching instantly?
  3. delay() blocks your entire program, which you learned in 2.6. If your button press happens during a delay(), what do you think happens to that button press?
  4. This is your first time reading a real physical input (the button) at the same time as running a real physical output (the LEDs). Why might timing your reads and writes correctly matter more now than it did with either task alone?

Notes

Look up these definitions:

  • Pushbutton
  • INPUT_PULLUP
  • Polling

Key idea: wire the same three real LEDs (red, yellow, green) from 2.6, each with its own resistor sharing one ground, plus a real pushbutton wired with INPUT_PULLUP. Check the button's state on every pass through loop() — called polling — and use it to flag a "pedestrian crossing" request. Rather than reacting mid-cycle (which delay()'s blocking behavior makes messy), let the sequence check for a pending request only at a safe point, like the start of the green phase. This is a hands-on preview of the input-handling ideas Unit 4 covers formally with if/else and interrupts.
