3.2 — Your First Real Circuit

🔔 Bell Ringer — Think About It
  1. Your TinkerCAD circuit for this same LED-and-resistor setup lit up exactly as calculated with zero fuss. What are two real reasons a physical circuit could fail to light up even when the math and the code are both completely correct?
  2. A multimeter reading of the actual current flowing through your circuit will almost never match your calculated prediction exactly. Is a small mismatch a sign something is wrong, or is it expected? Why?
  3. Real resistors are manufactured with a tolerance (often ±5%) — a "220 ohm" resistor might actually measure 209–231 ohms. Why would a manufacturer sell a part that isn't exactly what its label says?
  4. If you had to explain to someone why "it worked in the simulator" isn't the same as "it works," what's the single best real-world example from today to use?

Notes

Look up these definitions:

  • Breadboard (real)
  • Resistor tolerance
  • Measured value vs. calculated value

Key idea: rebuild your 3.2 TinkerCAD circuit — one LED, a 220 ohm resistor, a power source — for real, on a real breadboard. A breadboard's rows are electrically connected internally exactly like the simulated one, and LED polarity still matters the same way. The new step: measure the actual current flowing through your real circuit with a multimeter, and measure your resistor's actual resistance. Compare both real measurements against your Ohm's Law prediction. A small difference is normal — real resistors have a manufacturing tolerance, and a multimeter itself has a small margin of error. A large difference usually means a wiring mistake, not "real life being different from math."
