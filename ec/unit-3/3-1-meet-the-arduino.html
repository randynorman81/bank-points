3.1 — Meet the Arduino: Real Hardware

🔔 Bell Ringer — Think About It
  1. A multimeter reading and an analogRead() value describe the exact same real voltage, just in two different number systems (volts vs. 0–1023). Why would an engineer bother converting between the two instead of just picking one and always using it?
  2. Now that you're holding a real Arduino Uno instead of a simulated one, what's the first thing you notice is different from TinkerCAD that the simulation couldn't show you?
  3. A real potentiometer's actual resistance almost never matches its printed value exactly — TinkerCAD's simulated components are always perfectly exact. Why does that small, real-world imprecision matter to an engineer, even though it makes the math "messier"?
  4. If your simulated circuit worked perfectly in TinkerCAD but your real one doesn't light up at all, what's more likely to be the problem: your code, or your physical wiring? Why?

Notes

Look up these definitions:

  • Multimeter
  • Potentiometer
  • Analog voltage

Key idea: your Unit 2 work identified the Arduino Uno's parts in simulation — digital pins (0–13, HIGH/LOW only), analog input pins (A0–A5, read a range of voltage), power pins (5V, 3.3V, GND), the USB port, barrel jack, reset button, and the built-in LED on pin 13. Today you hold the real board and a real multimeter — a tool that measures real voltage, current, or resistance directly from a circuit. Turn a real potentiometer and measure the actual voltage across it at a few different positions, then read the exact same position with analogRead(). The multimeter gives you volts (0–5V); analogRead() gives you a raw number (0–1023) — two representations of the identical real signal.
