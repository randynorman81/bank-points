6.9 — Motor Types: Conceptual Overview

🔔 Bell Ringer — Think About It
  1. Your servo can only move within about 180 degrees and hold a position — it can't spin all the way around like a stepper or brushless motor can. Why would "less capable" actually be the right design choice for a door lock instead of a limitation to work around?
  2. If you'd picked a stepper motor instead of a servo for your door lock, what's one specific way that decision could have made your V2/V3 build worse, not just different?
  3. A brushed DC motor is simple and cheap but wears out because of physical contact; a brushless motor lasts longer but costs more and is more complex. If you were designing a product meant to last 10 years, which tradeoff would you accept, and why?
  4. "Motor selection" means picking the right tool for the actual task, not just whatever's available. Think of a time you, or someone you know, used the wrong tool for a job because it was just what was on hand — what went wrong?

Notes

Look up these definitions:

  • Stepper motor
  • Brushed DC motor
  • Brushless motor
  • Continuous rotation
  • Motor selection

Key idea: stepper motors move in precise, discrete steps and can rotate continuously in either direction — great for 3D printers/CNC where exact positioning matters. Brushed DC motors use physical contacts to deliver current — simple, cheap, common in toys, but the brushes wear out. Brushless motors replace brushes with electronic switching — more efficient and longer-lasting, common in drones and EVs. Your servo (6.6) is built specifically for position-holding within a limited range — the right tool for a lock needing exactly two held positions. A stepper would be overkill; a brushless motor (built for continuous rotation) would be the wrong tool entirely. Motor selection is choosing the right type for the actual task, not just whatever's available.
