7.5 — Case: Mirai Botnet

This case shows what happens when this unit's own concepts (connectivity, device ecosystems from 7.1) go wrong at massive scale.

Intro

Ask AI these questions; look over the answers.

  1. What was the Mirai Botnet, and how did it actually infect so many IoT devices?
  2. Explain to a middle schooler what a DDoS (Distributed Denial of Service) attack is, using something like a crowded doorway as an analogy.
  3. Create a simple diagram showing how individual infected IoT devices (cameras, routers) became part of a coordinated attack against a single target.
  4. Why were so many IoT devices vulnerable to this kind of infection in the first place? What specific security weakness made this possible at such scale?
  5. Connect today's case directly to what you learned in Lesson 7.1 about device ecosystems and connectivity — how did connectivity, the thing we said was "missing" from your own builds, become the actual problem here?
  6. What changed in the IoT industry's approach to device security after Mirai became widely known?

Notes

Look up these definitions and add them to your living glossary:

  • Botnet
  • DDoS (Distributed Denial of Service)
  • Default credentials
  • Attack vector

Background: in 2016, malware called Mirai infected hundreds of thousands of IoT devices (routers, cameras, DVRs) simply by trying factory-default credentials — not a sophisticated technique, just widespread failure to change default passwords. Infected devices became a botnet, used to launch a massive DDoS attack against Dyn (a DNS provider), knocking major websites offline for hours. This is the unit's central irony: connectivity, which we've described all unit as valuable and missing from your own builds, is exactly what made this possible — a standalone system with no connectivity could never join a botnet. Same pattern as Unit 5's UID-only authentication: a simple, preventable weakness causing large-scale real damage.
