Analog vs. Digital

The Big Idea

Question 1: what makes it analog or digital

Picture a ramp and a staircase that both climb to the same height. On the ramp you can stand at any height at all. On the stairs you can only stand on a step. That is the whole difference between analog and digital.

Analog

A continuous signal. It can be any value in its range, with no gaps, and it changes smoothly. The information is stored as a physical copy of the real thing: a groove shape, a liquid height, a magnetic pattern.

Digital

A discrete signal. It can only be certain set values, and underneath it's stored as numbers made of 1s and 0s. The real thing is measured over and over (sampled), and each measurement is saved as a number.

See the Difference

Questions 1, 2, 4

Turning something analog into digital means measuring it again and again and rounding each measurement to the nearest step. Drag the sliders: more samples and more steps make the digital copy closer to the real thing, but it never becomes perfectly smooth.

Samples per wave:

How often the value is measured (sample rate).

Steps (levels):

How many values each measurement can round to (bit depth).

Each extra bit doubles the number of steps: 1 bit = 2 levels, 2 bits = 4, 3 bits = 8, and so on.

Explaining It at Two Levels

Questions 3 + 4

Two of your answers explain the same idea to very different people. The facts stay the same. What changes is the words, the examples, and how much detail you use.

For a 4th grader

  • Use everyday comparisons: a ramp vs. stairs, a dimmer vs. a light switch, a clock with hands vs. a clock with numbers.
  • Short sentences. No technical words, or explain any you use.
  • One example they already know, like a thermometer or a song.

For someone with a CS degree

Use the real terms correctly:

  • Continuous vs. discrete signals
  • Sampling and sample rate (how often it's measured, in Hz)
  • Quantization and bit depth (how many levels each sample can be)
  • ADC / DAC: analog-to-digital and digital-to-analog converters
  • Noise, signal degradation, and lossless copying
05

analogRead() and digitalRead()

Questions 5 + 6 (everyone answers both)

The Arduino can listen to the world both ways. Some pins only hear "on" or "off." Others can hear everything in between. Slide the voltage and watch what each function would report.

Voltage on the pin:

analogRead(A0) returns

A number from 0 to 1023. 0 means 0V, 1023 means 5V. It uses the analog pins A0–A5 and the Arduino's built-in ADC (10 bits = 1024 levels).

digitalRead(2) returns

Only HIGH (1) or LOW (0). It uses the digital pins 0–13. Around 3V and up reads HIGH; around 1.5V and below reads LOW.

Where you'll use them: analogRead() is for things that change smoothly, like a light sensor, a temperature sensor, or a knob (potentiometer). digitalRead() is for things that are either one way or the other, like a button being pressed or not. Spelling: the real function names start with a lowercase letter: analogRead() and digitalRead().

Building Your Argument

Step 1 + Question 2

Your partner is arguing the other side with the same three topics. A strong argument doesn't just say "mine is better." It picks the qualities that matter most and shows evidence for them.

Quality to argue aboutAsk yourself
Accuracy / detailDoes it capture every tiny change, or round things to steps?
CopyingDoes a copy of a copy lose quality, or is every copy perfect?
Wear and damageDoes it wear out, scratch, or stretch each time it's used?
ConvenienceHow easy is it to store, carry, search, and share?
Power and dependenceDoes it need electricity, a device, or the internet to work?
ExperienceHow does it feel to use? Some people value warmth, collecting, or the ritual.
