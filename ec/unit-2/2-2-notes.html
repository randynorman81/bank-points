Getting Set Up in Tinkercad

2.2 Step 1 + 2.2A Part 1

Engineers build circuits in a simulator before touching real parts. In a simulator, a mistake costs nothing: no burned-out LEDs, no damaged boards. Tinkercad is where you'll do that all unit.

02

Inside the Breadboard

2.2 Steps 2 + 5, 2.2A Part 3 section 1

A breadboard looks like a grid of separate holes, but underneath, strips of metal connect certain holes together. Anything plugged into the same strip is connected. Click any hole below to light up everything it's connected to.

Click a hole.

Power rails (the long rows on the edges)

Each rail is one long strip that runs the whole length of the board. That way power (+) and ground (−) are available anywhere along the board. Wire the Arduino's 5V to the red (+) rail and GND to the black (−) rail.

Terminal rows (the middle)

Each numbered row is two short strips of 5 holes: a–e and f–j. The center divider (trench) splits them, so a part that straddles the gap has its two sides on different strips. That's how you add a second LED "across the divide" without connecting it to the first.

Same row = connected. If both legs of one part go in the same row on the same side of the divider (like a11 and b11), the metal strip connects them directly. Current takes that easy path instead of going through the part.

LEDs and Polarity

2.2 Steps 3 + 6, 2.2A Part 3 section 3

LED stands for Light Emitting Diode. A diode is a one-way street for electricity. Plug an LED in backwards and it isn't broken. It's just blocking traffic, exactly like it's designed to. Click the button to flip it.

04

What the Resistor Does

2.2 Steps 7 + 8, 2.2A Part 3 section 3

An LED on its own lets current rush through it almost unchecked. The resistor is the speed bump that keeps the flow at a safe level. Ohm's law tells you exactly how much current gets through.

I = V ÷ R current (amps) = voltage (volts) ÷ resistance (ohms). More resistance = less current.

Try it: resistance

A red LED uses about 2V of the 5V, so about 3V is left for the resistor. Current through it ≈ 3V ÷ R.

Most LEDs are rated for about 20 mA (0.020 amps). Far above that, the LED overheats and burns out. mA means milliamps: thousandths of an amp.
05

Two LEDs: Series or Parallel?

2.2A Part 2 + Part 3 section 2

In 2.2A, both LEDs get power from the same rails, but each one has its own path from + to −. Click an LED to unplug it and see what happens to the other one.

Series

One single chain: the current goes through one part, then the next. Break the chain anywhere, and everything goes off, like old holiday lights.

Parallel

Separate branches, each connecting + to − on its own. Each branch works independently. A rail lets one Arduino pin feed as many branches as you connect to it.

Tracing a path: start at the Arduino's 5V pin and follow the wire, rail, resistor, LED leg by leg, until you reach GND. Name each hole and part you pass through, in order.
06

The Fault Experiments

2.2A Part 4 (and 2.2 Step 8)

Breaking a circuit on purpose is one of the fastest ways to understand it. In a simulator, you can do it for free. Do each experiment yourself in Tinkercad and write down exactly what you see.

Experiment A: short circuit

A short circuit is a path with almost no resistance that lets current skip a part. Electricity always takes the easiest path available. If the ground wire lands in the same row as the resistor end, current has a path back to ground that never goes through the LED.

Experiment B: overcurrent

Replacing the resistor with a plain wire removes the only thing limiting the current (look back at section 04 with R near 0). Watch the LED for a warning symbol and hover your mouse over it to read Tinkercad's message. Then think about which LEDs share that path, and which have their own branch.

Stop the simulation before moving any wire, then start it again to test.

Test yourself

One-way LEDs, a resistor on every one, and a breadboard that is connected in strips.
