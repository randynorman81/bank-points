5.9 — Sprint Retrospective + V2 Planning

🔔 Bell Ringer — Think About It
  1. The notes say a retrospective claiming "everything went well, no problems" is rarely true and never useful. Why is admitting what went wrong actually more valuable to a team than only reporting what went right?
  2. You're asked to plan a V2 improvement today without building it. Why might separating "deciding what to build" from "actually building it" lead to a better final result than diving straight into code?
  3. Today you're also choosing your two strongest Forensics Reports from the whole semester. What makes a report "strong" beyond just getting a good grade, and is that the same thing as the report you personally learned the most from?
  4. This closes out the course's formal introduction to Scrum. Why might breaking a big project into short sprints, each ending with an honest look back, work better than one long build with no scheduled check-ins at all?

Directions

  1. Write your Sprint Retrospective answering all three questions with specifics.
  2. Plan (don't build) a specific V2 improvement.
  3. Submit both, plus "What I'd Build Next," in Google Classroom.
  4. Choose your 2 strongest Forensics Reports and write your My Forensic Analysis reflection (see Assignments below).

No new building today — bring your Lesson 5.6 code for genuine reflection. This closes out the unit's formal Scrum introduction.

Notes

No new vocabulary today.

A retrospective is a structured reflection at the end of a sprint. Three questions: (1) What went well? Be specific, not "it went fine." (2) What didn't go well? Be honest — naming friction isn't a confession of failure. (3) What would you do differently? Should connect directly to #2. A superficial retrospective ("everything went well, no problems") is rarely true and never useful. Today you'll also plan — not build — a genuinely new V2 improvement for your PIN system.
