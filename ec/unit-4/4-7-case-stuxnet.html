4.7 — Case: Stuxnet

Notes

Look up these definitions:

  • Malware
  • Falsified feedback
  • Centrifuge
  • Nation-state attack

Background: Stuxnet, discovered in 2010 and widely believed developed by U.S. and Israeli intelligence (never officially confirmed), targeted the industrial control systems running centrifuges at Iranian nuclear facilities. It caused the centrifuges to spin at damaging speeds while simultaneously feeding falsified feedback to operators showing everything normal — operators had no way to know anything was wrong until physical damage became undeniable. This is the most important detail of the case: a system that lies about its own status is more dangerous than one that simply breaks visibly. Contrast with Oldsmar (3.8): that attack was caught by a human watching a screen; Stuxnet was specifically designed so no one watching a screen would suspect a problem at all.

    Connected cases for Section 8: Ukraine Power Grid Attack (2015) — remote access to grid control systems caused immediate, visible outages for ~230,000 people, unlike Stuxnet's hidden, gradual damage. Colonial Pipeline Ransomware (2021) — ransomware on surrounding IT systems forced a precautionary pipeline shutdown, causing fuel shortages; the malware never directly controlled physical equipment. For Section 8: how did each attack achieve real-world disruption differently — direct manipulation with false feedback, direct remote control, or indirect precautionary shutdown? What does this reveal about the range of ways critical infrastructure can be attacked?
