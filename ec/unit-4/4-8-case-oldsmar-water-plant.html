4.8 — Case: Oldsmar Water Plant

🔔 Bell Ringer — Think About It
  1. A hacker changed a chemical setpoint by roughly 100 times its normal level, and the system accepted the change without flagging anything as suspicious. Why would a system controlling something as dangerous as drinking water chemicals not have a hard-coded safety limit that simply rejects an obviously insane number?
  2. The attack was only stopped because a plant operator happened to be watching the screen at that exact moment. What does it say about the state of critical infrastructure security if pure luck was the last line of defense?
  3. This is basically the same missing-input-validation problem as the Jeep Cherokee hack, but here the target wasn't one car — it was an entire community's water supply. Does the scale of who could get hurt change how seriously you think this kind of flaw should be treated, or is a security flaw a security flaw regardless of scale?
  4. If you were writing the code for a water treatment system, what specific line of logic could have stopped this attack automatically, without needing a human watching a screen at all?

Notes

Look up these definitions:

  • Industrial control system (ICS)
  • Setpoint
  • Critical infrastructure
  • Remote monitoring

Background: in February 2021, a hacker remotely accessed the industrial control system at a Florida water treatment plant and changed the sodium hydroxide setpoint from 111 parts per million to 11,100 — roughly a 100× increase, potentially lethal. A plant operator happened to be watching the screen and reversed it before it reached the water supply. The system accepted the change without flagging it as suspicious, despite being wildly outside any normal range — the same missing input-validation theme as the Jeep Cherokee case, but at the scale of an entire community's critical infrastructure. Human attention caught this, not an automated safeguard.
