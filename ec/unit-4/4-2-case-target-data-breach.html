4.2 — Case: Target Data Breach

🔔 Bell Ringer — Think About It
  1. The attackers didn't break into Target directly — they stole credentials from an HVAC vendor who had legitimate remote access. Why might trusting a smaller, less-secure outside company end up being the biggest security risk a large company faces?
  2. A point-of-sale terminal is, at its core, just an input device — like the keypad you're about to build. Does knowing that change how you think about the keypad project, now that you know real attackers specifically target the moment data gets typed in?
  3. Once attackers had a foothold in one part of Target's network, "lateral movement" let them work their way over to the payment terminals. Why might a company's internal network need walls between departments, not just one strong wall at the outside edge?
  4. Roughly 40 million card numbers were stolen through a vendor's HVAC access — literally the air conditioning system. What's the most unexpected "back door" you can imagine into a system you'd normally think of as completely unrelated?

Notes

Look up these definitions:

  • Point-of-sale (POS) terminal
  • Vendor access
  • Lateral movement
  • Data exfiltration

Background: in 2013, attackers stole network credentials from an HVAC vendor with legitimate remote access to Target's systems, then used lateral movement to reach the point-of-sale terminals themselves, installing malware that captured card data as customers swiped. Roughly 40 million card numbers and 70 million customer records were stolen; Target paid an $18.5 million settlement. A POS terminal is fundamentally an input device — just like the keypad you're about to build. This case is a direct preview of this unit's theme: what happens when the device accepting input is itself compromised?
