Application Software: What You Actually Install

The average phone has dozens of apps. Almost none of them are the same kind of thing. Learn the categories, and learn how to tell a safe download from a trap.

The Software Family Tree

Open your phone and scroll. A calculator, a group chat, a game, a camera, a bank, a music player. They feel like completely different things. To a computer scientist, they sort neatly into just three families.

Family 1

System software

Runs the computer itself: the OS, drivers, and utilities like antivirus and disk cleanup. You rarely open it on purpose.

Family 2

Application software

Software you use to get something done or have fun: browsers, games, Docs, Spotify, TikTok. This is what most people mean by “an app.”

Family 3

Development software

Tools used to make other software: code editors, IDEs, compilers, debuggers. (Lesson 5.9.)

02

Native, Web, or Mobile?

The same app can come in three different forms, with different tradeoffs.

Native app

Installed on your computer and built for one specific OS. Fast, can use your hardware fully, usually works offline. Examples: Photoshop, Minecraft, Microsoft Word desktop.

Catch: must match your OS and processor (Lesson 5.4).

Web app

Runs inside your browser; the real work happens on a server. Works on any OS with nothing to install. Examples: Google Docs, Canva, Figma, Gmail.

Catch: usually needs internet, and depends on a company's servers staying up.

Mobile app

Built for iOS or Android and installed from an app store, using phone features like the camera, GPS, and notifications. Examples: Instagram, Uber, Snapchat.

Catch: asks for permissions — which is where safety comes in.

03

Install Smart

Green flags

  • Official app store or the developer's own site
  • Lots of recent, specific reviews
  • Permissions match what the app does
  • A real company or developer with a history

Red flags

  • A flashlight app that wants your contacts and microphone
  • A “free” version of paid software from a random site
  • Pop-ups telling you to install something to “fix” your device
  • Installers bundled with extra programs you didn't pick
Permission check: a calculator does not need your location. When an app asks for something that doesn't fit its job, deny it — the OS (5.1, job #6) lets you.
Every app you install is a stranger you're giving a key to.Choose the ones that need the fewest keys.

Three families, three forms, one habit: check before you install.
