Ports, Bluetooth & NFC

Every device you own connects to the world two ways: through a physical cable, or over the air. This page covers both halves of 3.2 — the wired ports you plug in every day, and the two wireless technologies (Bluetooth and NFC) that quietly run your earbuds, your smartwatch, and every tap-to-pay you've ever done.

00

What Cables Actually Do

Every cable on the back of every device on Earth does one of two jobs — and usually a combination of both: it moves power (electricity from a wall outlet, battery, or computer, so a device can run or charge), and it moves data (files, video, audio, button presses, so devices can talk to each other).

If the job is just "move power and data," why are there so many shaped plugs? Cables were invented at different times, for different jobs, by different companies — each new shape solved a problem the old ones couldn't. Think of it like sports equipment: a bat, a racket, and a hockey stick all do a similar thing (hit something), but each is shaped for its specific game.

USB — Universal Serial Bus

The all-purpose cable. Charges your phone, connects your keyboard, transfers files, plugs in your headphones — a little of everything.

HDMI — High-Definition Multimedia Interface

The TV cable. One job: send high-quality video and audio from a source to a screen. That's pretty much all it does.

The Three Shapes of USB

Same name, three completely different plugs — each one built for a different job and a different era.

USB-A — The Classic Rectangle

The original USB plug, invented in 1996 — the flat rectangle on the side of most laptops and desktops for 25 years. It only fits one way; flip it and it won't go in (the classic "USB flip").

Connects: computers, keyboards/mice/controllers, flash drives, wall chargers.

Can't: charge most modern laptops, send video to a screen reliably, fit in upside-down.

USB-B — The Square-ish Printer Plug

A chunky, almost-square plug that looks like a tiny house with a sloped roof. Built thicker and sturdier on purpose, for devices that don't move around — most famously printers.

Connects: printers, scanners, older external hard drives, audio/MIDI gear.

Can't: plug into phones or most laptops directly, charge modern devices.

USB-C — The Modern Oval

The newest shape, slowly replacing all the others. Symmetrical — no upside-down, flip it any direction. One USB-C port can charge a laptop, send video to a 4K monitor, transfer huge files, and connect headphones, all at once.

Connects: modern phones, laptops made after 2018, tablets, headphones, the Nintendo Switch.

Catch: the shape is identical on every device, but not every USB-C port does every trick — some only charge, others also carry video and high-speed data.

HDMI — The Video & Audio Specialist

A flat, 6-sided trapezoid built for one job: moving high-quality video and sound from a source to a screen. It bundles picture and sound into a single cable — older TV setups needed two separate cables for that.

Connects: game consoles, streaming boxes, external monitors, Blu-ray players, projectors.

Can't: charge anything, plug into a USB port, carry data files like USB does.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

01

Forcing it in upside down

If a USB-A or HDMI cable doesn't fit, don't push harder — flip it. The pins inside are delicate and bend easily.

02

Assuming all USB-C is equal

The shape is identical everywhere, but capabilities vary — one port might charge AND carry video, another (cheaper) might only trickle-charge.

03

Trying to charge with HDMI

HDMI doesn't carry meaningful power — it's a video cable, not a charger.

04

Mixing up USB-B and USB-C

Both are "not USB-A," but they're completely different — USB-B is big and chunky for printers, USB-C is small and oval for modern devices.

Bluetooth: The Story Behind the Name

In 1996, three tech companies — Intel, Ericsson, and Nokia — were trying to agree on a single wireless standard so their devices could all talk to each other. Intel engineer Jim Kardach suggested a temporary code name: Bluetooth.

Why "Bluetooth"? The name comes from King Harald "Bluetooth" Gormsson, a 10th-century Viking king who united the warring tribes of Denmark and Norway into a single kingdom. Kardach saw the parallel: just as King Harald united rival tribes, this technology would unite rival communication protocols. The codename was supposed to be temporary — it stuck. Even the Bluetooth logo is a Viking rune, made by combining King Harald's initials (H and B) into one merged shape.

What Bluetooth CAN Do

  • Connect over short-to-medium range — up to ~30 feet (newer versions up to 300 feet)
  • Stream audio — wireless earbuds, headphones, car speakers
  • Stay paired — once connected, devices remember each other
  • Connect peripherals — keyboards, mice, controllers, fitness trackers
  • Run for hours on a small battery (especially Bluetooth Low Energy)

What Bluetooth CAN'T Do

  • Connect to the internet — device-to-device only
  • Stream HD video — not enough bandwidth
  • Reach reliably across a room — walls and bodies block it
  • Connect more than ~7–8 active devices at once
  • Transfer large files quickly — Wi-Fi is about 100x faster

Wait — Isn't Bluetooth Just Wi-Fi?

Both are wireless, but built for completely different jobs — like confusing a walkie-talkie with a phone.

NFC: Near Field Communication

The technology behind every "tap to pay" you've ever done. The name is literal — it's wireless communication that only works when devices are extremely close, usually within 4 inches (10 cm) or less.

Where NFC came from: developed in 2002 by Sony and Philips, built on older RFID technology used for decades in security badges and library books. Sony/Philips launched the NFC Forum in 2004 with Nokia to set standards. First NFC phones arrived in 2006, but it didn't really take off until Apple Pay launched in 2014 — the moment NFC became a household technology.

What NFC CAN Do

  • Connect instantly — no pairing, no passwords, just tap
  • Run without batteries — passive tags get power from the tap itself
  • Handle payments securely — Apple Pay, Google Pay, Samsung Pay
  • Read smart tags — stickers that launch apps or websites
  • Work as ID/access cards — school IDs, hotel keys, transit passes

What NFC CAN'T Do

  • Work at any real distance — both devices must be within ~4 inches
  • Transfer large files — ~0.4 Mbps, slower than Bluetooth
  • Stay connected — built for quick taps, not ongoing connections
  • Stream audio or video — far too slow
  • Connect through much — even a thick phone case can block it

Why Is NFC So Popular Now?

Wired or wireless, every connection was built to solve one specific problem.

USB-A made universal connections possible. USB-C fixed "which way is up" and added real power and speed. HDMI stripped video and audio down to one cable. Bluetooth unites devices for ongoing connections. NFC handles the one-tap moments. Knowing which is which means you'll never be stuck staring at a port, wondering what goes where.
