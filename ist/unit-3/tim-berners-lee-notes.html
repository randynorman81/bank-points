Tim Berners-Lee & the Birth of the Web

The scientist who connected the world's information — and then, at the exact moment he could have become one of the richest people alive, chose to give it away for free instead.

00

Before the Web: The Mess

It's the 1980s, at CERN — one of the world's biggest physics labs. The internet already exists. It just doesn't work the way you'd expect: it's like a library where every book is written in a different language and locked in a different, incompatible room.

Tim Berners-Lee was a scientist there, and he wasn't trying to change the world. He was just trying to solve his own team's information problem.

Data Silos

Scientists at CERN had information trapped on different computers that couldn't "talk" to each other at all.

Physical Barrier

To see a colleague's work, you had to learn their specific computer's commands — or physically walk to their desk and look at their screen.

No Connections

Information lived in total isolation. There was no way to "click" from one paper to a related one.

01

The Original Idea Was Small

Tim didn't set out to invent something that would run modern life. He just wanted two things:

Linking Research

A system where scientists could browse research results and notes across different computers as if they were one giant document.

Universal Access

A system that would work on any computer, regardless of brand or software — no exceptions.

02

How He Built It: The 3 Pillars

Working on a "NeXT" computer at CERN, Tim coded three core technologies — the exact three that let you read this page today.

HTML

The Skeleton

The language used to build and style web pages — the structure everything else hangs on.

HTTP

The Delivery Truck

The protocol that moves data from a server to your browser, every time a page loads.

URLs

The Address

The unique name that tells your computer exactly where to find one specific page.

03

The World-Changing Decision

In 1993, Tim made the single most consequential choice of his life. People around him urged him to patent the Web and charge money for it. If he had, he'd be one of the richest people who ever lived.

Instead, he and CERN released the source code for the World Wide Web into the public domain, for free. Anyone, anywhere, could build a website without asking permission or paying a cent.

That single decision is why the Web exploded. A small lab tool for physicists became the global force that runs modern life — because nobody had to ask permission first.
"This is for everyone."— Sir Tim Berners-Lee

Two very different webs

The Patented Web

Every website would owe Tim (or CERN) a license fee. Small creators, students, and hobbyists likely couldn't afford to build anything. Growth would be slow and controlled by whoever held the patent.

The Web We Got

Anyone with a computer could build a site for free. Millions did — which is exactly why the Web grew from a physics lab tool into something billions of people use every day.

Three simple technologies, one enormous decision to give them away.

HTML gave every page a structure. HTTP gave every page a way to travel. URLs gave every page an address. And the choice to make all three free, for everyone, is the entire reason the Web became the Web instead of staying a physics lab's internal tool.
