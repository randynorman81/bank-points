1.1–1.2 — CIA Triad, OSINT & Your Digital Footprint

CIA Triad
The three goals of keeping information secure:
  • Confidentiality — only the people who should see information can see it.
  • Integrity — information is accurate and hasn't been tampered with.
  • Availability — information is there and usable when you need it.
OSINT (Open Source Intelligence)
Gathering information from publicly available sources — things like social media, public records, and registries that anyone can access, no hacking required.

1.3 — Police, the 4th Amendment & Online Tracking

Police deception is legal
In Georgia (and most of the U.S.), police can legally use trickery, bluffing, and outright lies during questioning to get a confession — interrogations are designed to secure confessions, not to have an honest conversation.
The "Voluntary" Standard (O.C.G.A. § 24-8-824)
Police CAN lie about facts: fake evidence (claiming your DNA/fingerprints were found), false witnesses (claiming a friend already blamed you), and undercover identity (posing as a peer without revealing they're police). Police CANNOT lie about consequences: they can't promise a lighter sentence for talking (an illegal "hope of benefit"), threaten physical harm, or keep questioning you after you clearly invoke your right to remain silent or ask for a lawyer.
How to invoke your rights
Say clearly: "I am invoking my right to remain silent, and I want a lawyer." Then stop talking — no explaining, no casual conversation. Once said clearly, all legal questioning must stop. This can be invoked at any point, even mid-interrogation, but it can also be un-invoked by you if you choose to start talking again afterward.

1.4 — Your Phone's Invisible Footprint

The 3 categories of your digital footprint
Your phone constantly gives off information you never see. Location (records of where you've been), Habits (patterns in how and when you use your phone), and Hidden Data (info tucked inside files and apps that isn't shown to you). About 90% of crimes now leave some kind of digital trace behind.
Dennis Rader (BTK) — caught by Hidden Data
Evaded capture for over 30 years. He sent police a deleted file on a floppy disk, not realizing "deleted" doesn't mean gone — hidden data inside the file led police straight to him.
Bryan Kohberger — caught by Location + Habits
His phone showed no signal at all during the exact hours of the crime, and it had also been near the scene several times before — both a location record and a habit pattern helped build the case against him.

1.5 — Password Security & Hacking Ethics

Entropy
A measure of how unpredictable a password is to a computer — not how complicated it looks to a person.
Brute-force attack
Trying every possible character combination, one by one, until one works.
Dictionary attack
Starting from real words and leaked password lists, then applying predictable tweaks (capitalizing a letter, adding "123").
Leetspeak substitution
Swapping look-alike characters into a word, like @ for a (e.g. P@ssw0rd). It barely slows down cracking tools — they check these automatically.
Hash & rainbow table
Sites store a scrambled version of your password called a hash, not the password itself. A rainbow table is a precomputed lookup chart of hashes for common passwords, letting an attacker find yours instantly if it's common.
Credential stuffing
When one site gets breached, attackers try that same username + password on every other site they can think of — reusing a password turns a single leak into access to all of your accounts.
MFA (multi-factor authentication)
A second proof of identity beyond your password, like a code sent to your phone.
Keylogger
Software that secretly records every key you press and sends it to an attacker.
Passphrase
A string of random, unrelated words (like river-comet-anchor) instead of one word with substitutions.
Password manager
A tool that generates and remembers a unique password for every site behind one master password.
Crimes come in categories
The same basic act can be a different crime with a different penalty depending on things like intent and planning. Killing someone can be first-degree murder, second-degree murder, voluntary manslaughter, or involuntary manslaughter. Taking something can be petty theft, grand theft, robbery, or burglary. The category decides the consequence.
The three colors of access
Green = authorized: somebody said yes, in writing, before anything started. Amber = grey area: nobody meant harm, but nobody officially said yes either. Red = unauthorized: nobody said yes, no matter the reason — always illegal.
White hat hacker
Hired or officially invited to find security weaknesses. Only looks where allowed, and reports what they find so it can be fixed. Authorized (green).
Grey hat hacker
Breaks in without asking first but claims good intentions — testing, curiosity, or exposing a flaw. Still not authorized, no matter the reason. Legally risky (amber).
Black hat hacker
Breaks in without permission to steal, damage, spy, or make illegal money. Unauthorized (red).
Why the reason doesn't save you
A grey hat with good intentions and a black hat with bad intentions can be charged under the same law — it cares about whether you had permission, not about how good your reason sounded.

1.6 — Online Tracking & Common Security Problems

Incognito / private browsing
Does not make you anonymous to the websites you visit — they can still identify and track you.
Browser fingerprinting
Identifying you using unique details your device gives away — screen resolution, battery level, installed fonts — even after you delete cookies and hide your IP address.
Shadow profile
A profile a company builds on you even though you never signed up for that service — pieced together from other people's contacts, tags, and connections.
Real-time bidding
Opening an app can broadcast your location to about 1,000 companies at once, in under 50 milliseconds, as they bid to show you an ad — every company that "loses" the auction still keeps what it learned about you.
The FBI & data brokers
Police normally need a warrant to track your location. But since data brokers sell that same location data on the open market, agencies can buy it instead — no warrant required.
HTTPS
Adds encryption to a website connection that plain HTTP doesn't have.
CAPTCHA
Used to prove you're a human, not a bot.
2FA
Stands for Two-Factor Authentication.
OWASP
Focuses on web application security.

1.7 — Copyright & Ethics

Copyright
Protects original creative work — things like music, writing, and images.
Plagiarism / copyright infringement
Using someone else's creative work without permission or credit.
Fair use
Allows limited use of copyrighted material for purposes like education, commentary, and criticism.
Public domain
Work with no copyright restrictions — anyone can use it freely.
