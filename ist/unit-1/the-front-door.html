The Front Door

Think of every website like a building, with strangers walking up to the front door all day long. HTTPS, CAPTCHA, 2FA, and OWASP are the four things standing between those strangers and whatever's inside.

01 / TRANSIT

HTTPS

The lock on the connection itself — scrambles what you send so nobody along the way can read it.

02 / ENTRY

CAPTCHA

The doorman — checks that whoever's knocking is an actual person, not a computer program.

03 / IDENTITY

2FA

The second deadbolt — a stolen key by itself still isn't enough to get in.

04 / INSPECTION

OWASP

The building code — a list of the ways buildings like this one usually fail.

01

HTTPS — the lock on the connection

Plain HTTP (no "S") sends everything you type — your password, your messages, your search — as plain, readable text, like writing it on a postcard. Anyone else on the same network can read it as it travels by. HTTPS is the same thing, but locked in a sealed envelope first.

What that little padlock icon actually checks

Encryption
Your data gets scrambled into gibberish (using something called TLS) so it's unreadable to anyone spying on it in transit — including someone else on the same public Wi-Fi.
Certificate identity
Confirms you're actually talking to the real website in the address bar, not a fake server pretending to be it.

Here's exactly what the difference looks like — same website, same login, one small difference in the address bar that changes everything:

⚠ Not Securehttp://coolgame-freebies.net/login
What a snooper on the same Wi-Fi actually sees:username=jayden22&password=Fluffy123
🔒 Securehttps://coolgame-freebies.net/login
What that same snooper sees instead:4f3a9c8e2b7d1f6a9c3e8b2d7f1a4c9e5b8d2f7a1c...
Example: You're at a coffee shop typing your email password into a site with plain http://. Someone else on that same Wi-Fi, using free tools, could actually see your exact password as it travels through the air, like in the panel above. With https://, that same password looks like random scrambled junk to them instead.

Warning: the padlock is not a "trust me" badge

HTTPS only proves that nobody in the middle can read your connection to a site. It says absolutely nothing about whether the site itself is honest. A scammer can build a perfect fake copy of your bank's login page on a look-alike website address, and that fake page can have a valid padlock too.

02

CAPTCHA — the doorman

CAPTCHA is actually short for a mouthful: Completely Automated Public Turing test to tell Computers and Humans Apart. In plain English: its entire job is to slow down computer programs that are pretending to be a real person.

What "bots" (automated programs) can do that a human can't

Credential stuffing
Automatically trying millions of stolen username/password combos against a login page, all within minutes.
Ticket & sneaker bots
Buying out an entire concert or sneaker drop in the very first second, before any real person can even click "buy."
Spam & fake reviews
Flooding comment sections and review pages with fake posts faster than any human moderator could ever keep up with.
Example: Ever notice sneakers or concert tickets selling out in literally two seconds? That's usually not real people — it's often bots built specifically to out-click every human on the page.

5 kinds of CAPTCHA you've probably already seen:

1
Distorted text

Type the wavy, stretched-out letters and numbers exactly as shown. The oldest, most familiar kind — you've probably seen this one the most.

2
Image grid

"Click every square with a traffic light, crosswalk, or bicycle in it." Bots are still bad at recognizing real-world objects in messy photos.

3
The checkbox ("I'm not a robot")

You just click one box. Behind the scenes, it's secretly watching how your mouse moved and how you clicked to decide if you're human.

4
Invisible / behind-the-scenes

No visible test at all. It silently scores your behavior on the page the whole time, and only shows you a real test if something looks suspicious.

5
Slider or puzzle piece

Drag a puzzle piece into the missing spot in a picture, or slide a bar all the way to one side, using a smooth, human-like motion.

There's also an audio version on most sites — it reads a code out loud instead of showing an image, for anyone who can't see well.

03

2FA — the second deadbolt

People who study security sort every way of proving "this is really you" into three categories, called factors. Two-Factor Authentication (2FA) just means using two of them together, so a leaked password by itself isn't enough to get in.

Something you know

Password / PIN

The weakest factor on its own — it can be guessed, leaked in a breach, or tricked out of you (phished).

Something you have

Phone / security key

A physical object an attacker would also have to actually steal from you.

Something you are

Fingerprint / face

Biometric, meaning tied to your actual body — not something you can memorize or hand off.

5 real examples of 2FA, ranked weakest to strongest:

1
SMS text code

Can be stolen through "SIM-swapping" — an attacker tricks your phone carrier into moving your phone number onto their own phone.

Weakest
2
Email verification code

A code gets emailed to you instead of texted. If your email password is already weak or reused, this barely adds any protection at all.

Weak
3
Authenticator app

Creates a rotating code right on your device — there's no phone call or text message for an attacker to intercept.

Better
4
Push notification approval

Your phone gets a pop-up asking "Was this you? Approve or Deny." Fast and easy — just don't approve one you don't recognize.

Better
5
Security key or fingerprint / Face ID

A physical key (like a YubiKey) that must be plugged in or tapped, or your own fingerprint/face on your device — both are extremely hard to steal remotely.

Strongest
Example: Two people both have their passwords leaked in the same breach. One only uses a password — the attacker logs right in. The other has 2FA turned on with an authenticator app — the attacker has the password but gets stuck, because they don't have that person's actual phone too.
04

OWASP — the building code

OWASP stands for Open Web Application Security Project — a big name for a simple idea. It's just a group of computer safety experts who write down the most common mistakes that get websites hacked, then hand that list to anyone building a website, so they know exactly which mistakes to avoid.

Think of it like this: if burglars kept breaking into houses the same three ways, a smart home inspector would write those three ways down and hand the list to every builder in town, so new houses stop repeating the same mistakes. OWASP does exactly that, except for websites instead of houses.

Their most famous list is the OWASP Top 10 — literally the 10 biggest, most common mistakes, ranked from worst to less-worst. Two are worth knowing by name:

#1
Broken access control

The single most common mistake on the whole list — a user can see or change stuff they were never supposed to be allowed to touch, often just by changing one number in a web address.

#3
Injection

A website asks you to type something normal, like your name, but never checks what you actually typed before using it — so if you type a trick phrase instead, the website might just obey it like a command.

Example (broken access control): Say your grades live at a web address like school.com/grades/1042, where 1042 is your student ID number. If the site never double-checks whose grades you're actually allowed to see, just typing 1043 instead could show you a completely different student's grades.
Example (injection): Imagine a website is like a genie that grants exactly whatever you type into a box, no questions asked. Normally you'd type a name, like "Alex." But if someone types a sneaky command instead of a name, the website "genie" might grant that command too — even something like "delete every student's data." A safe website always double-checks what you typed first, instead of blindly obeying it.

OWASP isn't a company and it isn't the government — it's just the checklist that real engineering teams actually build against before they let a website go live.

05

Inspect the building

Six example sites below. Before you read the finding, try to decide for yourself which of the four locks is missing, working correctly, or actively being tested by an attacker.

Site A

A shopping site's address bar shows http://, not https://, and it's asking for a credit card number.

FindingNo HTTPS — that card number would travel as plain, readable text, visible to anyone else on the same network.
Site B

A login page lets a computer program attempt ten thousand password guesses in a single second, with no slowdown at all.

FindingNo bot protection — a CAPTCHA or a simple speed limit on login attempts would have stopped this immediately.
Site C

Someone's password leaks in a data breach, but the attacker still can't log in — the account also asks for a code from that person's phone.

Finding2FA working exactly as designed — the leaked password alone wasn't enough to get in.
Site D

A search box lets visitors type raw commands, and the server just runs whatever it's given, no questions asked.

FindingExactly the kind of flaw OWASP warns about — this is called an injection vulnerability.
Site E

Someone gets a text asking them to "confirm" a 2FA code for a login they never actually tried to make.

FindingA live attack happening right now — someone already has the password and is trying to get past the second lock too.
Site F

The address bar shows a padlock and https:// — but the web address is a near-perfect misspelling of a real, well-known bank.

FindingHTTPS is present, but the site is still fake — remember, the padlock never promised the site itself was legitimate.
Four locks, four completely different jobs. None of them cover for the other three — a website can pass three inspections perfectly and still fail badly on the fourth.

Try this

  • Check the lock. Visit any website you use daily and click the padlock icon in the address bar. Read exactly what it claims to verify.
  • Time a CAPTCHA. Notice how much less often you're asked to click little images than you were a few years ago. What do you think changed?
  • Look up your own 2FA. Check one account's security settings — is your second factor a text message, an app, or a physical key?

Talk about it

  • If a fake site can still get a valid HTTPS padlock, what's actually stopping scammers from using one on their fake site too?
  • Why do you think broken access control — just changing a number in a web address — is the single most common flaw on OWASP's whole list?
  • Is it worth losing a few seconds to a CAPTCHA on every single site, if it stops bots on all of them combined?
  • Why would a company choose to publish its own security weaknesses publicly, the way OWASP catalogs them for the world to see?

None of these locks were built to stop a person. They were built to stop everything else.

HTTPS, CAPTCHA, 2FA, and OWASP all exist because the internet is full of things that aren't people at all — scripts, bots, and scanners moving at a scale no lock on a real, physical door ever had to deal with. Knowing what each one actually checks is how you can tell a locked door apart from a truly safe one.
