Caught by Their Own Data

Eight real cases where the technology that gave someone away was something they did not know existed, forgot about, or never thought mattered. Work through each case file, then test yourself.

A word about these cases. Several of them involve real victims and real families. We study them to understand how digital evidence works, not for shock value, and the notes leave out graphic detail on purpose. Everything here comes from court records and major news coverage. Where reports disagree, the case file says so.
00

The pattern

Look for the same thing in every case: the person was careful about the things they knew could be traced, and got caught by something they did not think about. A hidden field in a file. The gap where a phone should have been. A cousin's DNA test. An old forum post. A photo. A printer. A step counter.

Then notice the flip side. The same invisible trail that catches a criminal can clear an innocent person (Case 8), can be wrong (Case 7), and exists for every one of us. That is why the 4th Amendment fights in lesson 1.3 matter.

The fair-warning part: this is about you too

Law enforcement used these tools legally in these cases, and courts are still deciding where the limits are. In Carpenter v. United States (2018) the Supreme Court ruled that police generally need a warrant to get a long stretch of your phone's historical cell-tower location records. After the Golden State Killer case, the genealogy site GEDmatch changed its rules so users must opt in before police can search their DNA.

Now audit yourself. Check each one you honestly do not know the answer to.

Discuss: If a technology can identify a killer and also track every ordinary person, who should decide when it can be used? A judge? A law? You, by choosing what to switch on?
