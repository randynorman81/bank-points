BRIEFING 1.5  /  SUBJECT: PERMISSION

Rules of Engagement

The exact same computer skill can be a real paid job, or a federal crime. The only thing that decides which one it is: one piece of paper. Did somebody with the actual authority actually say "yes, go ahead" first?

Green

Authorized

Somebody said yes, in writing, before anything started. This is legal hacking — a real job people get paid to do.

Example: A company signs papers saying "please try to break into our app for two weeks."

Amber

Grey area

Nobody meant any harm, but nobody officially said yes either. This is risky, even when the goal was a good one.

Example: Finding a real problem on a website by accident, then not being sure if you're even allowed to mention it.

Red

Unauthorized

Nobody said yes — no matter what the reason was. This is illegal. Full stop, every time.

Example: Guessing a friend's password just to look around their account, even if you never change anything.

01

White hat, grey hat, black hat

The word "hacker" by itself doesn't actually tell you whether someone broke the law. The color of the "hat" is just a simple way people describe the answer to one single question: did they have permission?

White hat

● Authorized

Hired, or officially invited, to find weak spots in a system. They only look where they're allowed to look, and they report what they find so it can get fixed.

Example: A company pays someone to try to break into their website for two weeks, then that person hands over a full report of every problem they found.

Grey hat

● Legally risky

Breaks in without asking first, but says they meant well — testing security, exposing a flaw, or just being curious. Still not authorized, no matter the reason.

Example: Finding a way into a school website without permission just to see if it's possible, then emailing the school afterward to say "hey, I found a way in."

Black hat

● Unauthorized

Breaks in without permission to steal something, cause damage, spy on people, or make illegal money. This is the kind that ends up in news stories.

Example: Breaking into a store's computer system to steal customers' credit card numbers.

Here's the part that surprises most people: why someone did it barely changes anything, legally speaking. A grey hat with genuinely good intentions and a black hat with bad intentions can both be charged under the exact same law — because that law cares about whether you had permission to be there, not about how good your reason sounded.

02

What "penetration testing" actually is

A "penetration test" — most people just call it a "pen test" — is a real job. A company actually pays someone to try to break into their own computer systems on purpose, before a real criminal gets the chance to do it for real.

What makes it legal: the scope agreement

A "scope agreement" is just a signed paper that spells out exactly what's allowed. Four things it always has to include:

Signed before testing starts
The paper has to be signed before any testing happens. Testing first and asking for permission afterward is already breaking the law.
Exact systems named
The paper lists precisely which systems can be tested. Anything not on that list is completely off-limits — even if it belongs to the exact same company.
Time window set
The paper names exact dates. Testing before those dates, or after them, is no longer authorized.
Reporting required
Whatever gets found has to be reported privately to the company first — never posted online, never sold, never used for anything else.
Example: A company hires someone to test their website for two weeks in June. If that same person also pokes around the company's private email system (which wasn't listed in the agreement), or keeps testing in July after the agreement ended, both of those are now illegal — even though it's still the exact same company.

Step outside that scope, even by accident, and you're no longer authorized — agreement or not.

03

Hack the Pentagon & bug bounties

In 2016, the U.S. Department of Defense (the Pentagon) tried something that sounds wild at first: they invited real, background-checked hackers to try to break into official government websites on purpose, and paid them for every real problem they found. It was literally called "Hack the Pentagon."

hackers signed up for the pilot program
138

$75K

It worked so well that the Pentagon kept running programs like it for other systems too. It proved something big companies had been slow to admit: paying hackers to find problems on purpose is actually cheaper — and safer — than waiting around for a criminal to find those same problems first.

Example: Paying a hacker a $3,000 reward to find and report one serious problem is a great deal. That exact same problem, found later by a criminal instead, could cost a company millions of dollars and every customer's trust.

Today, websites like HackerOne and Bugcrowd run "find the bug, get paid" programs for thousands of real companies. Anyone can join, look for a real security problem inside the posted rules, and get paid legally for finding it — some people even make this their entire career.

04

The law doesn't care about your reason

The main U.S. law here is called the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, or CFAA for short. It was passed all the way back in 1986, before most people even owned a home computer. It makes it a serious federal crime to get into a computer system without authorization — and the rule is stricter than most people expect.

You don't have to break anything

Just looking around inside a system you weren't allowed into — even if you change nothing, take nothing, and mean zero harm — can already be a crime. It's not about the damage you cause. It's simply about whether you were allowed in or not.

Example: Finding out a classmate's password and logging into their school account "just to look around," then logging right back out and changing nothing at all — that's still against the law, because nobody ever gave you permission to log in.

These excuses feel reasonable, but none of them actually work as a real legal defense — meaning none of them would convince a judge:

Not a legal defense

"I was just curious." · "I didn't take anything." · "I was going to tell them eventually." · "I have a right to know."

What actually matters

Did the actual owner of the system say yes — in advance, in writing — to exactly what you did?

For a student, the real-world stakes are bigger than they sound: logging into a school computer system without permission can mean getting expelled from school and facing serious federal criminal charges at the same time — even when nothing was ever damaged or stolen.

Good intentions don't add a real signature to a real permission form. If nobody who actually has the authority to say yes ever said it, the answer is red — no matter how good it felt, or how good the reason sounded from the inside.

Try this

  • Find a real bounty. Look up HackerOne's public program list online. Pick one real company and read exactly what's "in scope" (allowed) and "out of scope" (not allowed).
  • Read a real scope agreement. Search "penetration testing rules of engagement template" and see exactly how specific the boundaries actually get.
  • Trace Hack the Pentagon. Find one news article about it and pull out one detail that isn't already on this page.
  • Spot the excuse. Next time you hear someone say "I was just looking," decide for yourself: would that actually hold up as a real excuse in court?

Talk about it

  • If someone finds a real problem with no safe or legal way to report it, what should they actually do?
  • Why does the law punish unauthorized access even when nothing was ever stolen or broken?
  • Is someone who leaks real evidence of wrongdoing doing something different from a black hat? Legally? What about ethically — is that a different question?
  • Why do you think companies were slow to start paying hackers, instead of just trying to keep every hacker out completely?

The skill was never the crime. The permission was always the whole case.

Every ethical hacker started out as someone who was simply able to break in. What turns that skill into a career instead of a felony (a serious crime) is scope, permission, and paperwork — signed before anything happens, not how good the reason sounded afterward.
