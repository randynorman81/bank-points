BRIEFING 1.5  /  SUBJECT: AUTHORIZATION

Rules of Engagement

The exact same skill can be a paid job or a federal crime. The only thing that changes is one piece of paper: did anyone actually say yes?

Green

Authorized

Permission was given, in writing, before you started. This is legal hacking — a real, paid career.

Amber

Grey area

No malicious intent, but no clear permission either. Legally risky, even when the goal was good.

Red

Unauthorized

No permission, whatever the reason. This is illegal, full stop, regardless of intent or outcome.

01

White hat, grey hat, black hat

"Hacker" doesn't tell you if someone did something legal. The color of the hat is really just shorthand for one question: did they have permission?

White hat

Hired or explicitly invited to find weaknesses, working inside an agreed scope, and reports what they find so it can get fixed.

● Authorized

Grey hat

Breaks in without permission but claims good intent — testing security, exposing a flaw, or just curious. Still not authorized.

● Legally risky

Black hat

Breaks in without permission to steal, damage, spy, or profit. This is the kind that makes the news.

● Unauthorized

Motive changes almost nothing legally. A grey hat who means well and a black hat who means harm can be charged under the exact same law — because that law is about access, not intent.

02

What penetration testing actually is

A penetration test ("pen test") is a real job: a company hires someone to break into their own systems, on purpose, before a real attacker does.

What makes it legal: the scope agreement

Signed before testing starts
Written permission has to exist first — testing, then asking, is already a crime.
Exact systems named
The agreement lists precisely what can be tested. Anything outside that list is off-limits.
Time window set
Testing only counts as authorized during the dates in the agreement.
Reporting required
Findings go to the company privately first — not posted publicly, not sold, not used.

Step outside that scope — even on a system owned by the same company — and you're no longer authorized, agreement or not.

03

Hack the Pentagon & bug bounties

In 2016, the U.S. Department of Defense ran "Hack the Pentagon" — the federal government's first bug bounty program, openly inviting vetted hackers to find flaws in official government websites.

hackers registered for the pilot program
138

$75K
paid out to hackers in the first pilot alone

It worked so well the Pentagon kept running bounty programs for other systems. It proved something companies had been slow to admit: paying hackers to find flaws on purpose is cheaper — and safer — than waiting for a criminal to find them first.

Today, platforms like HackerOne and Bugcrowd run bounty programs for thousands of companies. Anyone can join, find a real vulnerability inside the posted rules, and get paid legally — some researchers make a full career out of it.

04

The law doesn't care about your reason

The main U.S. law here is the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA), passed in 1986. It makes accessing a computer system without authorization a federal crime — and it's stricter than most people expect.

You don't have to break anything

Just looking around a system you're not authorized to access — even if you change nothing, take nothing, and mean no harm — can already be a crime. Unauthorized access is the crime, not the damage.

Not a legal defense

"I was just curious." · "I didn't take anything." · "I was going to tell them." · "I have a right to know."

What actually matters

Did the owner of the system say yes, in advance, in writing, to exactly what you did?

For a student, the real-world stakes are bigger than they sound: unauthorized access to a school network can mean expulsion and felony charges under the CFAA or a state equivalent — even when nothing was damaged and nothing was stolen.

05

Classify the operation

Same skillset, six different situations. Read each one and think about which color you'd stamp on it before you scroll past.

Scenario

A company runs a public bug bounty program; a hacker finds and reports a real bug, exactly within the program's posted rules.

Red
Good intentions don't add a signature to a scope agreement. If nobody with the authority to say yes actually said it, the color is red — no matter how it felt from the inside.

Try this

  • Find a real bounty. Look up HackerOne's public program list. Pick one company and read exactly what's in scope and out of scope.
  • Read a real scope agreement. Search "penetration testing rules of engagement template" and see how specific the boundaries actually get.
  • Trace Hack the Pentagon. Find one news article about it and pull out one detail that isn't on this page.

Talk about it

  • If someone finds a flaw with no way to legally report it, what should they actually do?
  • Why does the law punish unauthorized access even when nothing was stolen or broken?
  • Is a hacktivist who leaks real evidence of wrongdoing doing something different from a black hat? Legally? Ethically?
  • Why do you think companies were slow to start paying hackers instead of just trying to keep them out entirely?

The skill was never the crime. The permission was always the whole case.

Every ethical hacker started as someone who could break in. What makes it a career instead of a felony is scope, permission, and paperwork, signed before anything happens — not how good the reason sounded afterward.
