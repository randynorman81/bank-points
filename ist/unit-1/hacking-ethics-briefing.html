BRIEFING 1.5  /  SUBJECT: PERMISSION

Rules of Engagement

The exact same computer skill can be a real paid job, or a federal crime. The only thing that decides which one it is: one piece of paper. Did somebody with the actual authority actually say "yes, go ahead" first?

00

Every Crime Has a Category

Almost no crime is just one single charge. The law breaks most crimes into categories based on things like intent, planning, and how much harm was caused — and which category applies changes the penalty completely. Killing someone isn't automatically "murder." Stealing something isn't automatically "grand theft." Here's what that actually looks like for two of the most common categories of crime.

Categories of killing someone
Most severe

First-Degree Murder

Intentional and planned out in advance. Usually the harshest penalty a state allows — up to life in prison.

Examples
  • Buying a weapon days ahead specifically to kill someone as planned
  • Poisoning someone's food over several days until it kills them
  • Paying another person to kill someone on your behalf
Very severe

Second-Degree Murder

Intentional, but not planned ahead of time — the intent to kill or seriously hurt someone formed in the moment.

Examples
  • Shooting someone in a sudden argument, with no planning beforehand
  • Beating someone to death in a spontaneous fight, intending real harm
  • Firing a gun into a crowded room, killing someone, without aiming at anyone
Serious

Voluntary Manslaughter

An intentional killing in the heat of the moment, after real provocation, with no time to "cool off" first.

Examples
  • Killing someone seconds after walking in on a serious betrayal
  • A fatal blow struck during a fight that erupted only moments earlier
  • Killing an attacker after the real danger had already passed
Least severe

Involuntary Manslaughter

No intent to kill at all — caused by reckless or seriously careless behavior instead.

Examples
  • Causing a fatal crash while texting and driving
  • A worker's death caused by a supervisor ignoring known safety rules
  • Fatally mishandling a gun believed (wrongly) to be unloaded
Categories of stealing
Misdemeanor

Petty Theft

Taking property below a state's dollar-value cutoff. Usually a fine or under a year in jail.

Examples
  • Shoplifting a $30 shirt from a store
  • Taking a classmate's phone case without asking
  • Sneaking snacks out of a store without paying
Felony

Grand Theft

Taking property above that same cutoff. Sentences can run into multiple years.

Examples
  • Stealing a car
  • Taking a $1,500 laptop from an unlocked office
  • Walking out of a store with an expensive, unpaid-for watch
Felony

Robbery

Taking property directly from a person using force or the threat of force.

Examples
  • Demanding someone's wallet at knifepoint
  • Snatching a phone from someone's hand while threatening them
  • Forcing a store clerk to open the register at gunpoint
Felony

Burglary

Unlawfully entering a building with intent to commit a crime inside — even if nothing is actually taken.

Examples
  • Breaking into a house while the owners are away to take valuables
  • Sneaking into a locked school at night to steal equipment
  • Prying open a store's window after hours to steal merchandise

Exact definitions and sentencing ranges vary by state — these are the general categories most U.S. legal systems use.

Same pattern, this lesson's actual subject: unauthorized computer access works exactly the same way. The exact same technical skill can be a paid job or a federal crime — and just like homicide and theft, which category it falls into changes everything about the consequences.
Green

Authorized

Somebody said yes, in writing, before anything started. This is legal hacking — a real job people get paid to do.

Example: A company signs papers saying "please try to break into our app for two weeks."

Amber

Grey area

Nobody meant any harm, but nobody officially said yes either. This is risky, even when the goal was a good one.

Example: Finding a real problem on a website by accident, then not being sure if you're even allowed to mention it.

Red

Unauthorized

Nobody said yes — no matter what the reason was. This is illegal. Full stop, every time.

Example: Guessing a friend's password just to look around their account, even if you never change anything.

01

White hat, grey hat, black hat

The word "hacker" by itself doesn't actually tell you whether someone broke the law. The color of the "hat" is just a simple way people describe the answer to one single question: did they have permission?

White hat

● Authorized

Hired, or officially invited, to find weak spots in a system. They only look where they're allowed to look, and they report what they find so it can get fixed.

Example: A company pays someone to try to break into their website for two weeks, then that person hands over a full report of every problem they found.

Grey hat

● Legally risky

Breaks in without asking first, but says they meant well — testing security, exposing a flaw, or just being curious. Still not authorized, no matter the reason.

Example: Finding a way into a school website without permission just to see if it's possible, then emailing the school afterward to say "hey, I found a way in."

Black hat

● Unauthorized

Breaks in without permission to steal something, cause damage, spy on people, or make illegal money. This is the kind that ends up in news stories.

Example: Breaking into a store's computer system to steal customers' credit card numbers.

Here's the part that surprises most people: why someone did it barely changes anything, legally speaking. A grey hat with genuinely good intentions and a black hat with bad intentions can both be charged under the exact same law — because that law cares about whether you had permission to be there, not about how good your reason sounded.

Same act, different category — and the category is what decides the consequence.

Killing someone, taking something, breaking into a computer: none of them are ever just one single charge. What separates a paid job from a felony is which box the exact same action falls into — and for hacking, that box comes down to one question: did somebody with the authority to say yes actually say it, first?
