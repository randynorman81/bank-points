The Copyright Case File

You didn't write it, film it, draw it, or code it — so are you allowed to post it, copy it, or turn it in for a grade? Here's exactly how to know, plus every mistake people make without even realizing it's a mistake.

CASE 01

What copyright actually covers

You don't apply for copyright, and you don't need a © symbol anywhere. The exact moment you make something — write it, film it, record it, save it — it's automatically protected. No forms, no fee, no waiting period. It just happens.

🛡 Protected

  • A song you recorded
  • A photo you took
  • An essay, story, or poem you wrote
  • Fan art, digital art, or a comic you drew
  • Code you wrote for an app or game
  • A video, TikTok, or Reel you filmed and edited
  • A Minecraft build or Roblox game you designed

🚫 Not protected

  • Facts (the sky is blue)
  • Ideas alone (a story idea about a superhero)
  • Titles, names, or short phrases
  • Methods or processes (how to bake bread)
  • Work whose copyright already ran out
  • A dance move or trend, by itself
  • A game's rules (just the "how you play" part)

Here's the part that trips people up: copyright protects the exact thing you made — the specific words, notes, or pixels — not the idea behind it. Two totally different people can each write their own story about a vampire falling in love. That's completely fine, and it happens all the time. The idea is free for anyone to use. It only becomes a problem if one person copies the other's exact sentences.

Example: Thousands of people post "satisfying slime" videos or "get ready with me" videos. The idea and the format are free for anyone to copy. But if someone re-uploads your exact video, shot-for-shot and word-for-word, and pretends they made it — that's a real problem.

Owning the copyright hands you a bundle of exclusive rights — meaning you're the only one who gets to do these things, or say yes/no when someone asks:

📋
Copy it
📤
Share it
👁
Display it
🎵
Perform it
✂
Remix it

Copy it = make more copies. Share it = distribute or post it. Display it = show it publicly. Perform it = play or perform it out loud. Remix it = change it into something new (also called making a derivative work). Only the copyright owner can legally do any of these — or give someone else permission to.

CASE 02

The fair use test

Fair use lets you use a piece of someone else's copyrighted work without asking permission first — but only if it passes a 4-part test. Courts (and platforms like YouTube) weigh all four factors together. No single one automatically wins the whole argument by itself.

1

Why are you using it?

Is it transformative — meaning you added your own commentary, a joke, a lesson, a review — or are you just reposting it exactly as-is, for fun or to get views?

2

What kind of work is it?

Factual work (news, data, a Wikipedia article) gets more leeway than highly creative work (a song, a movie, a novel).

3

How much did you use?

A short 5-second clip to make a point is much safer than using the whole song, scene, or article from start to finish.

4

Does it hurt sales?

Could someone watch or listen to your version instead of paying for the real thing? That's the factor courts and platforms weigh the heaviest of all four.

CASE 03

Public domain & Creative Commons

Not everything needs permission first. Some work is free to use because the copyright already expired. Other work is free because the creator chose, on purpose, to give some of their rights away.

Public domain means nobody owns the copyright anymore — not the original creator, not anyone. In the U.S., this usually happens automatically to very old work (generally anything published before 1929), or when a creator officially gives up all their rights (a dedication). Anything made by the U.S. federal government — like NASA photos — is automatically public domain too, which is why you can use any NASA image you want, for anything, for free.

Creative Commons (CC) is different. The creator still legally owns the copyright — they never gave it up. But they've pre-approved certain kinds of reuse in advance, as long as you follow their exact license terms.

CC BY

Attribution

Use it for anything — even to make money — just credit the creator by name.

Example: a photographer says "use my photo anywhere, just tag me."
CC BY-SA

Share-Alike

Credit the creator, and license your remix under this exact same license.

Example: Wikipedia's own text uses this license.
CC BY-NC

Non-Commercial

Credit the creator, but don't sell it or use it to make money.

Example: free for a personal video, not for a paid ad.
CC0

Public Domain Dedication

The creator waives every right on purpose. Use it however you want.

Example: same freedom as true public domain.
Watch out for this trap: "royalty-free" does not automatically mean "free to use." It usually just means you pay once instead of paying every single time. Always read the actual license before assuming something is free — don't guess based on the label alone.

Legit free-to-use sources:

  1. Unsplash & Pexels — free stock photos
  2. YouTube Audio Library — free music & sound effects
  3. Wikimedia Commons — images, mostly CC or public domain
  4. NASA Image Library — U.S. government, public domain
  5. Pixabay — free photos, illustrations, and video clips
  6. Freesound.org — free sound effects, CC-licensed
CASE 04

Where teens actually break the rules

Nobody wakes up planning to commit infringement. It happens in totally normal, everyday moments, without anyone realizing it's even a rule. Here's exactly where it usually starts.

Scenario · social video

Using a trending song, full and unedited, as background audio on a public post.

Verdict: Platform-licensed only Apps like TikTok or Instagram pay for a license that lets you use certain songs inside their app. That permission doesn't travel with the video — the moment you download it and repost it somewhere else, the license is gone.
Scenario · school project

Grabbing an image from Google Images for a poster or slideshow.

Verdict: Risky habit Usually totally fine for a private classroom assignment your teacher grades. It becomes a real problem the moment that project gets posted somewhere public, like a website or social media.
Scenario · reposting art

Sharing someone's digital art or photo with no credit, or worse, claiming you made it.

Verdict: Infringement Reposting without credit already hurts the creator. Claiming it as your own adds a second, separate problem on top — that part is also plagiarism.
Scenario · fan art & fan fiction

Drawing or writing new stories using someone else's characters.

Verdict: Technically infringement, usually tolerated Most creators and studios quietly allow non-commercial fan work. The moment someone tries to sell it, though, that tolerance disappears fast.
Scenario · a movie/show "edit"

Screen-recording a scene from a movie or show, adding music and captions, and posting it as your own "edit."

Verdict: Usually infringement Adding music and captions doesn't automatically make it transformative enough to count as fair use, especially with a long, unedited clip. Most "edit" accounts stay up simply because nobody has reported them yet — not because it's actually allowed.
Scenario · AI-generated art

Asking an AI image tool to make art "in the style of" a specific living artist, then posting it as your own.

Verdict: Unsettled & contested Many AI companies trained their tools on real artists' work without asking, and artists are actively suing over it. Separately, the U.S. Copyright Office says a fully AI-generated image can't be copyrighted by anyone at all — so it was never really "yours" to claim as your own work in the first place.
CASE 05

Plagiarism vs. copyright: not the same crime

These two get mixed up all the time. They usually happen together, but they're actually judged by two completely different groups of people, for two completely different reasons.

⚖️ Copyright infringement

  • A legal issue between the creator and the person using their work
  • Applies even if you fully credit the source
  • Can lead to takedowns, demonetization, or lawsuits

🎓 Plagiarism

  • An ethics/academic issue between you and your school
  • Solved by citing your source — copyright still applies even after you cite it
  • Can lead to a zero, a discipline referral, or worse
Example 1: Copying three paragraphs from Wikipedia into your essay with no citation is plagiarism at your school, no matter what — whether or not Wikipedia's content happened to be free to reuse in the first place.
Example 2: If that source you copied wasn't actually free to reuse, you could have both problems at once — a copyright problem and a plagiarism problem, running side by side, judged by two different people, for two different reasons.
CASE 06

What actually happens if you get it wrong

DMCA takedown

The platform removes your post after the copyright holder files a DMCA claim — no lawsuit required, and it can happen within hours.

Demonetized or muted

YouTube keeps the video up but strips the ad money, or silently mutes just the audio track.

Account strikes

Repeated claims against the same account can get it suspended or permanently banned.

Academic consequences

At school, plagiarism or claiming someone else's work as your own is a zero and a conduct issue — completely separate from any copyright claim.

Legal action (rare, but real)

Actual lawsuits are uncommon for typical teen use, but they do happen — almost always for large-scale or repeated commercial infringement, not a one-time school project.

Most creators aren't trying to catch you doing something wrong. The takedown, the mute, the zero — those all happen automatically, whether you meant to break the rule or not.

Try this

  • The source check. Pick any image on your phone right now that you didn't take. Could you explain, in one sentence, why you're legally allowed to have it?
  • The four-factor test. Take a video you've seen reposted or reacted to online. Walk it through all four fair-use factors yourself, one at a time.
  • The license hunt. Find one photo on Unsplash or Wikimedia Commons and actually read its exact license terms before using it.
  • The CC decoder. Search "Creative Commons" images on Google or Flickr, and see if you can correctly guess each license type before clicking to check.

Talk about it

  • If a platform auto-licenses a song for in-app use, why doesn't that license travel when you download and repost the video somewhere else?
  • Is fan art "stealing," or is it free advertising for the original creator? Does the answer depend on who the fan artist is?
  • Should AI companies have to pay artists whose work trained the model? Who should get to decide that?
  • Why does citing a source stop plagiarism, but it doesn't stop copyright infringement?

Somebody made everything you didn't make.

Copyright isn't a trap waiting to catch you — it's the deal that lets creators actually get paid for their work. Knowing the four fair-use factors, spotting public domain and CC-licensed work, and knowing the difference between plagiarism and infringement means you get to use the internet's creative work responsibly — instead of accidentally getting it wrong.
