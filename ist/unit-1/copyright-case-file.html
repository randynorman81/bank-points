The Copyright Case File

You didn't write it, film it, draw it, or code it — so can you post it, remix it, or turn it in? The rules that decide, and the ones people break every single day without knowing it.

CASE 01

What copyright actually covers

Copyright isn't something you apply for. The second you write it, film it, record it, or save it, it's automatically protected — no © symbol, no registration, no fee.

🛡 Protected

  • A song you recorded
  • A photo you took
  • An essay, story, or poem you wrote
  • Fan art, digital art, comics
  • Code you wrote for an app or game

🚫 Not protected

  • Facts (the sky is blue)
  • Ideas alone (a story about a superhero)
  • Titles, names, short phrases
  • Methods or processes (how to bake bread)
  • Work whose copyright has expired

Copyright protects the specific expression — the exact words, notes, or pixels — not the underlying idea. Two people can both write a vampire romance novel; only the exact text of each one is protected.

Owning the copyright hands you a bundle of exclusive rights:

📋
Copy it
📤
Distribute it
👁
Display it
🎵
Perform it
✂
Remix / adapt it

Only the copyright holder can legally do these things — or give someone else permission to.

CASE 02

The fair use test

Fair use lets you use a piece of someone else's copyrighted work without asking — but only if it holds up against four factors. Courts weigh all four; no single one automatically wins.

1

Purpose & character

Is it transformative — commentary, criticism, parody, education — or are you just reposting it as-is for entertainment or profit?

2

Nature of the work

Factual work (news, data) gets more leeway than highly creative work (a song, a movie).

3

Amount used

A short clip to make a point is safer than using the whole song, scene, or article.

4

Effect on the market

Does your use replace the need for anyone to pay for the original? That's the factor courts weigh heaviest.

How it plays out in real posts:

A parody accountMaking fun of a movie trailer using your own jokes over a few short clips.

CASE 03

Public domain & Creative Commons

Not everything needs permission. Some work is free to use because copyright expired; other work is free because the creator chose to give some rights away.

Public domain — copyright has expired (in the U.S., generally anything published before 1929) or the creator released it with no restrictions. Work made by the U.S. federal government — like NASA photos — is automatically public domain too.

Creative Commons (CC) — a creator keeps copyright but pre-approves certain reuse, as long as you follow the license terms:

CC BY

Attribution

Use it for anything, just credit the creator.

CC BY-SA

Share-Alike

Credit the creator, and license your remix the same way.

CC BY-NC

Non-Commercial

Credit the creator, don't sell it or profit from it.

CC0

Public Domain Dedication

Creator waives all rights. Use it however you want.

Legit free-to-use sources:

  1. Unsplash & Pexels — free stock photos
  2. YouTube Audio Library — free music & sound effects
  3. Wikimedia Commons — images, mostly CC or public domain
  4. NASA Image Library — U.S. government, public domain
CASE 04

Where teens actually break the rules

Nobody sets out to infringe copyright. It happens in ordinary moments — here's where it usually starts.

Scenario · social video

Using a trending song, full and unedited, as background audio on a public post.

Verdict: Platform-licensed only Apps like TikTok license some music for in-app use — that license doesn't travel if you download and repost the video elsewhere.
Scenario · school project

Grabbing an image from Google Images for a poster or slideshow.

Verdict: Risky habit Usually fine for a private classroom assignment; becomes a real problem the moment that project gets posted publicly online.
Scenario · reposting art

Sharing someone's digital art or photo with no credit, or worse, claiming you made it.

Verdict: Infringement Reposting without credit hurts the creator; claiming it as your own adds a second, separate ethics problem on top.
Scenario · fan art & fan fiction

Drawing or writing new stories using someone else's characters.

Verdict: Technically infringement, usually tolerated Most creators/studios quietly allow non-commercial fan work — but the moment someone tries to sell it, that tolerance disappears fast.
Scenario · AI-generated art

Asking an AI image tool to make art "in the style of" a specific living artist, then posting it as your own.

Verdict: Unsettled & contested Artists are actively suing AI companies over training data. Separately, the U.S. Copyright Office says purely AI-generated images can't be copyrighted by anyone — so it was never really "yours" to claim.
CASE 05

Plagiarism vs. copyright: not the same crime

These two get mixed up constantly. They usually overlap, but they're judged by completely different people, for completely different reasons.

⚖️ Copyright infringement

  • A legal issue between the creator and the user
  • Applies even if you fully credit the source
  • Can lead to takedowns, demonetization, lawsuits

🎓 Plagiarism

  • An ethics/academic issue between you and your school
  • Solved by citing your source — copyright still applies even after you cite it
  • Can lead to a zero, a discipline referral, or worse

Copying three paragraphs from Wikipedia into your essay with no citation is plagiarism, whether or not Wikipedia's content happens to be free to reuse. The two rules run side by side, not instead of each other.

CASE 06

What actually happens if you get it wrong

DMCA takedown

The platform removes your post after the copyright holder files a claim — no lawsuit required.

Demonetized or muted

YouTube keeps the video up but strips the ad revenue, or silently mutes the audio track.

Account strikes

Repeated claims can suspend or permanently ban an account.

Academic consequences

At school, plagiarism or claiming someone else's work as your own is a zero and a conduct issue — separate from any copyright claim.

Most creators aren't trying to catch you. The takedown, the mute, the zero — those all happen automatically, whether you meant to break the rule or not.

Try this

  • The source check. Pick any image on your phone right now that you didn't take. Could you say, in one sentence, why you're legally allowed to have it?
  • The four-factor test. Take a video you've seen reposted or reacted to online. Walk it through all four fair-use factors yourself.
  • The license hunt. Find one photo on Unsplash or Wikimedia Commons and read its exact license terms before using it.

Talk about it

  • If a platform auto-licenses a song for in-app use, why doesn't that license travel when you download and repost the video?
  • Is fan art "stealing," or is it free advertising for the original creator? Does it depend on the fan artist?
  • Should AI companies have to pay artists whose work trained the model? Who decides?
  • Why does citing a source stop plagiarism but not stop copyright infringement?

Somebody made everything you didn't make.

Copyright isn't a trap waiting to catch you — it's the deal that lets creators get paid for their work. Knowing the four fair-use factors, spotting public domain and CC-licensed work, and knowing the difference between plagiarism and infringement means you get to use the internet's creative work responsibly, instead of accidentally.
