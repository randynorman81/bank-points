Who's watching you

Your phone is a microphone, a camera, a 3D scanner, an altitude gauge and a motion tracker. All of it runs whether or not you have an app open.

Phone sensors  /  app permissions  /  the data market  /  what to do about it

Layer 1

Hardware

Built-in sensors, always on. Your accelerometer can identify you by the way you walk.

Layer 2

Software

The apps you install and the permissions you hand them. Instagram's Friend Map broadcasts exactly where you are.

Layer 3

The market

Brokers who buy and resell you. Federal agencies buy location data instead of getting a warrant.

Most people only think about the middle one.

1

The hardware

Five sensors, each with a reason to exist and a way to be turned against you

Every sensor has two faces. There's the reason Apple put it in the phone, and there's what an app can do with it once you tap "Allow."

LiDAR scanner

Camera and AR apps
Why it's there

Focuses six times faster in the dark, so night photos come out sharp.

What it can do to you

Scans the 3D layout and furniture of your bedroom.

Barometer

Always on
Why it's there

Tells 911 what floor you're on inside a 50-story building.

What it can do to you

Estimates your family's income from whether you live in the penthouse or the basement.

TrueDepth camera

Face ID and filters
Why it's there

The hardest passcode there is to crack. It keeps your phone locked to your face.

What it can do to you

Reads micro-expressions to work out whether an ad made you happy or bored.

Ambient light sensor

Always on
Why it's there

Dims the screen so you don't fry your eyes and drain the battery.

What it can do to you

Tells an app whether you're lying in bed or standing outside.

Accelerometer

Always on
Why it's there

Detects a high-speed crash and calls for help on its own.

What it can do to you

Identifies you by the rhythm of your walk, which is as individual as a fingerprint.

This is not a conspiracy theory. Apple's own developer documentation describes LiDAR capturing 3D meshes of indoor spaces. Peer-reviewed studies have shown that iPhone accelerometer data can identify a specific person from their walking pattern. And the FCC's Z-axis mandate legally requires your phone to report vertical location to first responders.
2

The apps

Social apps used to settle for your city. Now they want your room.

TikTok

Scanning before you ever post

TikTok scans content during the pre-upload stage. Record a video and delete the draft, and your faceprint and voiceprint have already been captured. It happens the moment you hit record, not the moment you hit post.

The app also wants precise GPS rather than just your IP address, so its "Nearby" feed can push real foot traffic toward specific shops. Your location is inventory now.

Instagram

Encryption removed from DMs

Meta is rolling back end-to-end encryption on Instagram DMs. Until now, even Meta couldn't read your messages. Afterward, every DM is a postcard: readable by Meta, scannable by their AI, and available to law enforcement on request.

The new Friend Map feature needs constant precise location so friends can watch each other move in real time.

So why would Meta give up encryption? The official answer is low adoption. The unofficial one is simpler: encrypted messages can't be scanned to train AI, can't be turned into ad targeting, and can't be handed to police without a warrant. Removing encryption fixes all three problems at once, for Meta.
3

The market

Your data isn't shared like a file. It's auctioned.

Open any app with an ad and a packet of your data is broadcast to about a thousand companies in 50 milliseconds. That's roughly one blink.

1 winner 999 losers, all of whom keep the data anyway

  1. You open a weather app in a psychiatrist's waiting room

    Your GPS coordinates and advertising ID go out to roughly a thousand companies before the ad has even loaded on screen.

  2. A pharmacy wins the auction

    It pays the most and gets to show you an ad for vitamins. The system worked exactly as designed.

  3. The other 999 lost, and kept everything

    They didn't win, but they received the packet. They now know you were at a psychiatrist's office, and that fact goes into your file permanently.

The warrant loophole

Normally the government needs a judge to sign a warrant before it can track your movements. But this data is commercially available, and anyone with a credit card can buy it. So agencies argue no warrant is needed. They simply buy it, like any other customer.

Your app sells to a broker› The government buys from the broker› No warrant was ever required

The Fourth Amendment gets skipped, not because the law changed, but because a private company stood in the middle.

4

Lock it down

Ten minutes, no cost. Flip a switch as you finish each one.

You can't disappear, and pretending otherwise is a waste of your time. What you can do is make yourself expensive to follow.

Turn off Precise Location for social apps

Settings › Privacy & Security › Location Services

Go through Instagram, TikTok, Snapchat, X and Facebook one at a time. They'll still get your general area, but not your room. This is what blocks Friend Map and the Nearby feed from learning your address.

Turn off Motion & Fitness for social apps

Settings › Privacy & Security › Motion & Fitness

Switch it off for every social app. This is the one that shuts down gait identification.

Stop your carrier from selling you

Settings › Cellular › Limit Data Broker Sharing

Major US carriers sell your movement data to brokers by default. Toggling this on tells them to stop, and it is the single most powerful setting on this page.

Turn off tracking across apps

Settings › Privacy & Security › Tracking

Switch off "Allow Apps to Request to Track." That kills the advertising ID feeding the whole auction system, and every future tracking popup gets denied automatically.

Audit every permission you've granted

Settings › Privacy & Security › Camera, Microphone, Photos, Contacts, Bluetooth

Work down each category. If a flashlight app has microphone access, something is wrong. Revoke anything an app doesn't need in order to function.

These reduce how much leaks out. They don't stop it. Every app you install is one more exit door, so the real decision happens before you tap "Get."

5

See it yourself

Nine free sites that prove all of the above on your own device

Tap any card to open it. Try each one in a normal tab, then again in Incognito, and compare. Sometimes Incognito changes nothing at all.

Start here

01

Cover Your Tracks

From the Electronic Frontier Foundation. A plain-English report on how well your browser blocks trackers and ad networks.

coveryourtracks.eff.org
02

AmIUnique?

Compares your browser against millions of others and tells you how identifiable you are, with no cookies involved. Expect something like "unique among the 3.4 million we've seen."

amiunique.org

Eye-openers

03

Webkay

One page that guesses your location, your hardware, and which sites you're logged into right now.

webkay.robinlinus.com
04

Device Info

Lists nearly every sensor and spec on your device. Battery, GPU, screen size, installed fonts, connected speakers. Harmless one at a time; together they're a fingerprint.

deviceinfo.me
05

Clickclickclick

Narrates every move you make: clicks, scrolling, hesitation, hovering. A voice reads it out loud and gets creepier in real time. You'll laugh, then stop laughing.

clickclickclick.click
Everything these sites show you is information every website you already visit has access to. No hacking, no malware, no special permissions.

The Incognito myth

06

Nothing Private

Type your name in a normal tab. Open the site again in Incognito. It greets you by name.

nothingprivate.gkr.pw
07

Fingerprint.com demo

Generates a Visitor ID that usually survives both Incognito and clearing your cookies. This is the real commercial technology ad networks and fraud systems run on.

demo.fingerprint.com
Incognito was never about hiding from websites. It hides your history from other people using your computer. Sites can still identify you with around 99% accuracy through fingerprinting, even if you never log in.

Go deeper

08

BrowserLeaks

The actual techniques laid out one by one: IP, Canvas fingerprinting, WebGL fingerprinting, hardware leaks, each with an explanation of how it works.

browserleaks.com
09

CreepJS

Detects whether you're on a VPN or faking your identity, and scores how trustworthy your browser looks. A VPN can make you more identifiable, because its claimed location clashes with your timezone and language.

abrahamjuliot.github.io/creepjs
Use the exact CreepJS address above and no other. The developer publishes it only on GitHub Pages. Copycat sites at other domains run modified versions of the code and exist to capture the fingerprint data they pretend to show you. Which is a fitting way to end the unit: even the tool that teaches you about fingerprinting has impostors trying to fingerprint you.

Try this

  • The Clickclickclick challenge. Open it and try to make the narrator stop talking. You can't. If a site can do that in 30 seconds, what can one you use for six hours a day do?
  • The fingerprint compare. Run AmIUnique on two devices you own. If you're identifiable on both, are you anonymous on either?
  • The permission audit. Open Location Services right now. How many apps have Precise Location on that have no business with it?

Talk about it

  • If your browser is unique among three million, how much did clearing your cookies actually accomplish?
  • What's the difference between a tracker, a cookie and a fingerprint?
  • If Incognito doesn't hide you from websites, what was it built for?
  • Should a site be legally required to tell you when it's fingerprinting you?
  • If a stranger followed you all day, wrote down every place you went and scanned every room you entered, would that be fine? Because that's the deal you've already agreed to.

If the app is free, the price is the map of your life.

Your hardware is a witness that never sleeps. Privacy isn't a switch you flip once. It's a trade you make every time a page loads. The goal was never to disappear. It's to know exactly what you're handing over, and to be the one deciding.
