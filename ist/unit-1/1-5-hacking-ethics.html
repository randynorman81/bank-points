1.5 — Hacking Ethics

🔔 Bell Ringer — Think About It
  1. If someone breaks into a company's website, finds a serious security flaw, changes nothing, and immediately emails the company to report it — have they still committed a crime? Why or why not?
  2. A company can legally pay someone to try to hack them. Does that mean hacking itself was never really the crime — only the missing permission was? What does that tell you about how the law defines "wrong"?
  3. If a hacker breaks into a hospital's system to expose a real safety flaw that could kill patients, but never asked permission first, should their good intent matter as much as the law says it doesn't?
  4. The same law that convicts a malicious hacker also technically applies to a curious student who guesses a friend's password "just to look." Is that fair — or should a rule just be a rule, no matter the scale?

Extra Credit: Draw It Out (25 pts)

Take today's ideas off the screen and put them on paper. Pick one format and make it by hand:

  • One-pager / infographic — a single page that visually breaks down one full set from the notes: all three colors of access, or all three hacker hats, or all four categories of one crime (killing or stealing).
  • Comic strip (4–8 panels) — show the same action playing out as different crimes depending on intent, planning, or permission. For example: the same "someone takes a laptop" scene drawn as petty theft, then as robbery, then as burglary — or the same "someone gets into a computer system" drawn as green, then amber, then red.
  • Poster — a "Know Your Hats" or "Know Your Colors" poster that could actually hang in a computer lab.
Pencil, pen, or marker on paper — no computer, no printed clip art, no AI-generated images. Hand-drawn is the whole point.

To earn the full 25 points, your piece must:

  • Cover a complete set (all 3 colors, all 3 hats, or all 4 degrees of one crime), with every item labeled and defined in your own words.
  • Give a concrete example for each item (from the notes or your own).
  • Make the big idea land visually: the same act changes category based on intent, planning, or permission.
  • Be neat enough to read, use color on purpose (not just decoration), and have your name on it.
