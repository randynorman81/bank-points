The CIA Triad

Three questions you can ask about any information: is it secret, is it accurate, is it there?

LEARNYou already do cybersecurity

You lock your phone. You check your bank balance. You get annoyed when the school Wi-Fi drops before a deadline. Every one of those moments is really about one of three things, and security people call them the CIA triad (the CIA here has nothing to do with the spy agency). The three letters stand for three questions you can ask about any piece of information.

PillarThe questionAn analogy
ConfidentialityIs it secret?A diary with a lock, or a sealed letter
IntegrityIs it accurate?The safety seal on a food container
AvailabilityIs it there when I need it?The power and water at your house
Every security decision, from picking a stronger password to backing up your files, protects at least one of the three pillars. When you can name the pillar, you can reason about the tradeoff instead of just feeling that something online is unsafe.

LEARNConfidentiality: only the right people

Confidentiality means making sure only the right people can see your information. It is about privacy. You are the one who decides who counts as the right people.

ControlHow it protects confidentiality
Phone passcode or Face IDLocks out parents, friends and strangers from your texts and photos
Private Instagram or TikTok accountOnly approved followers see your posts
School portal loginKeeps your grades away from every other student
Disappearing Snapchat photoBuilt to be seen once, by one person: confidentiality with an expiration date
Incognito (private) browsingHides your search history from the next person who uses the same shared device, and nobody else
Two-factor authentication (2FA)A text code that stops someone from logging in with only your stolen password
Encrypted messaging (iMessage, Signal)Scrambles a message so only the right device can read it

Confidentiality is not only digital. What you tell a school counselor is protected by the same idea in a conversation instead of a login.

When it fails: a hacker guesses your password and reads your DMs, or a friend grabs your unlocked phone and screenshots a private chat. The notes name the second one as the fastest way confidentiality really breaks for a teenager: not a hacker, just a phone left unlocked in the wrong hands.

LEARNIntegrity: accurate and untouched

Integrity means your data is accurate and nobody who should not touch it has changed it. It is about trust. It is the pillar people think about least, until it breaks.

ExampleWhy it is an integrity question
Your grade in the school portalIt must match exactly what your teacher entered
Google Docs version historyCatches someone quietly deleting or rewriting a teammate's paragraph
A screenshot used as proofThe image itself can be edited before it is sent
A verified checkmarkHelps confirm the account really belongs to who it claims
Wikipedia editsAnyone can change a page, which is why teachers tell you to check sources
A text thread forwarded without the restEven without editing one word, leaving out context changes what someone really said
AutocorrectA harmless, accidental integrity problem: your message changed without your permission
A deepfake video or Photoshopped photoA deliberate attack on what you believe is real
When it fails: a hacker gets into an account and changes or deletes things, or someone edits a text before forwarding it to start a rumor. Either way you can no longer trust that what you are looking at is what was really there.

LEARNAvailability: there when you need it

Availability means the data or service is there and working at the moment you need it. It is about access. A system can be perfectly private and perfectly accurate and still be useless if it is down.

ExampleWhat happened
School Wi-Fi down before a deadlineThe network is not gone, just not there when you needed it
A streaming or social app outageThe app and your account are fine but unreachable
A game server crash mid-matchThe service was not there at the moment it mattered
Ransomware on a school's computersThe files are not deleted, just locked until someone pays
A DDoS attackSo much fake traffic arrives that real visitors cannot get through, so the site is "up" but unreachable
Your phone dying with a QR ticket on itThe ticket exists but is not available to you right then
A power outage taking down your routerThe oldest availability failure there is
When it fails: a hospital system hit with ransomware cannot pull up patient records. The data still exists. It just is not reachable.

LEARNWhich pillar failed? A three-question method

Use the same three questions every time you read about an incident. Ask what happened to the information itself, not how scary the story sounds.

  1. Did someone see it who should not have? Then confidentiality failed.
  2. Was it changed, faked or wrong? Then integrity failed.
  3. Could the right people not reach or use it? Then availability failed.

An incident can break more than one pillar. Ransomware that also steals a copy of the files breaks availability (locked) and confidentiality (copied). Name every pillar that applies and say which one hurt most.

Do not decide by who did it or how clever the attack was. Decide by what happened to the information. A leaked database, a changed grade and a crashed site are three different failures even if the same hacker caused all three.

LEARNThe three pillars pull against each other

A strong security setup balances all three pillars. Pushing one pillar too hard often costs another.

SettingWhat it gainsWhat it costs
Records locked so tightly that a doctor cannot open them in an emergencyConfidentialityAvailability
A single shared password for everyone so nobody gets locked outAvailabilityConfidentiality and integrity
Three approvals before any change to payment dataIntegrity and confidentialitySpeed, so some availability

There is rarely a setup that maxes out all three at once. Good security is choosing the right balance for the situation, not chasing a perfect score on every pillar. A hospital should lean toward availability for emergency records. A bank vault should lean toward confidentiality.

When someone proposes a new security rule, ask two questions: which pillar does it protect, and which pillar does it cost?

LEARNWhat is OSINT?

When most people hear "intelligence" they picture spies. The reality is less dramatic and more interesting. OSINT stands for Open Source Intelligence: gathering information from publicly available sources, such as websites, social media, news, public records, photos and videos. Anything anyone can reach without hacking, stealing or breaking a rule counts.

Open source does not mean leaked or hacked. It means the information is available to anyone who knows where to look. A Google search is OSINT. Looking up someone's LinkedIn is OSINT. The skill is not finding the data. The skill is knowing what to do with it once you have it.

Before a hacker tries to break into a system, they almost always do OSINT first. Once you understand OSINT you can use it on yourself: see what strangers can already find and clean up the footprint you did not know you had.

LEARNThe five main sources

None of these need hacking. All of it is technically public.

SourceWhat it isExamples
Search engines and the open webGoogle, Bing, DuckDuckGo, and engines like Shodan that search internet-connected devicesPDFs, employee names, forgotten pages
Social mediaPeople post location, workplace, routine and family on their ownInstagram, TikTok, LinkedIn, X, Facebook, YouTube, Reddit, Discord
Public records and government databasesMost are public by law, many free to search by nameCourt records, property deeds, marriage licenses, business filings, voter rolls, police blotters
News, blogs and archivesOld information that is still thereLocal newspapers, school newsletters, obituaries, the Wayback Machine
Data aggregators and people-search sitesScrape all of the above into one profileFastPeopleSearch, Spokeo, BeenVerified, Whitepages
The combined effect. Any one source can look harmless. Put all five together and a stranger can build a very complete picture of you in under an hour, without hacking anything.

LEARNWho uses OSINT, and why intent is the only difference

OSINT is a tool, not a weapon, like a kitchen knife. The same techniques that help a journalist expose corruption can help a stalker find a home. The information is identical. The intent is what changes.

GroupHow they use it
JournalistsVerify stories and uncover things powerful people want hidden, from the Panama Papers to war-crime documentation
Law enforcementFind missing persons and identify suspects in viral videos, often with no warrant because the data is already public
Cybersecurity professionalsRun OSINT on their own company to find what an attacker would see first, then fix it
EmployersHiring managers Google candidates, so a post from 9th grade can affect a job offer
Friends, family and datesA first date will probably Google you. This is OSINT even if nobody calls it that
ScammersLearn a boss's name and schedule, then send a fake email that looks real: spear phishing
Stalkers and doxxersFind a home address, school and family; doxxing is built almost entirely on OSINT

LEARNFive public posts: how Jordan gets profiled

Jordan has never been hacked and never had a data breach. Jordan just posts normally. Here is what a stranger can learn from five completely public Instagram posts.

PostWhat a stranger learns
"Friday night lights with my school's team!"Jordan's school, which day the games are, and a tagged photo of friends
A dog photo with a street sign visibleThe dog's name (often used in passwords), the neighborhood, and the exact GPS location if the original photo file is shared with its hidden EXIF data

In under twenty minutes, with zero hacking, a stranger knows Jordan's full name, school, neighborhood, family, schedule, car and common password material. None of it was stolen. All of it was given away.

Many apps strip EXIF data when you post, but sending the original photo file may not. Turn off location tagging for your camera if you do not want the coordinates saved at all.

LEARNOSINT and the CIA triad

OSINT pushes on all three pillars from lesson 1.1.

PillarWhat happensReal example
ConfidentialityEvery piece of public data is by definition not confidential. OSINT is built on the slow erosion of privacyA private thought you posted at 14 is still Googleable at 25
IntegrityPublic sources are full of errors, so an investigator can report something confidently wrongA typo in a public arrest record names the wrong person

OSINT is the biggest reason cybersecurity is hard in the modern world. Every post, public record and leaked email is a free clue for whoever looks. Security is not only firewalls. It is understanding the trail you leave behind.

LEARNWhere each pillar breaks for registries and blotters

These collections show all three pillars pulling against each other.

PillarThe conflictReal-life consequence
ConfidentialityTheir whole purpose is to make private information publicYour home address can be found on a people-search site for free; a minor old offense can show up forever, even after charges were dropped
IntegrityThe data must be true to be fair to the people listed, but it is scraped, copied and re-shared automatically, so errors spreadA typo lists the wrong name for an arrest; two people with similar names get mixed up and it takes years to fix
AvailabilitySociety wants the data always online for safety, but individuals want the right to be forgotten when it is wrongIf a registry site crashes, parents cannot check their neighborhood; someone proven innocent finds a mugshot nearly impossible to remove, and some sites charge a fee

There is no easy answer. For: blotters keep communities informed, registries help parents protect kids, records promote transparency. Against: errors ruin innocent people's lives, old mistakes follow people forever, and companies profit off your data without consent.

LEARNDefend yourself: OSINT hygiene

The best defense against OSINT is knowing what someone could already find. The tools cost nothing and take about an hour to learn.

  1. Search your own name in quotes, and your username, on several search engines.
  2. Check the social media accounts you have, including old ones, and tighten the privacy settings.
  3. Look at what your photos reveal: street signs, house numbers, school logos and location tags.
  4. Turn off location tagging for the camera.
  5. Look for yourself on people-search sites, and use their opt-out pages where they exist.
  6. Think before you post a schedule, a birthday or a vacation.
You cannot become invisible, and that is fine. The goal of OSINT hygiene is to make sure that what is out there about you is something you chose to put there.

LEARNThe 4th Amendment and the interrogation room

The 4th Amendment protects you from unreasonable searches and seizures, but it never defines what "unreasonable" means. What is legal for police to say and do during questioning surprises almost everyone the first time they learn it.

In Georgia, and most of the U.S., police may legally use trickery, bluffing and outright lies during questioning to get a confession. An interrogation is designed to secure a confession. It is not designed to be a casual, honest conversation. Officers build cases. That is their job in the room. It is not personal, and it is not necessarily unfair under the law.

No one in the room is going to explain your rights as the questioning goes on. You need to know them before you walk in.

LEARNThe voluntary standard

Georgia law (O.C.G.A. section 24-8-824) draws a line between what an officer may do and what makes a confession involuntary, which means it can be thrown out.

Police CANExample
Fake evidenceClaim your DNA or fingerprints were found at the scene when they were not
False witnessesSay a friend already blamed you when nobody said anything
Undercover identityPose as a peer, buyer or seller, online or in person, without saying they are police
Police CANNOTName
Make promisesOffer a lighter sentence or freedom for talking: an illegal hope of benefit
ThreatenUse physical harm or illegal consequences: a fear of injury
Ignore a clear invocationKeep questioning after you clearly say you want to stay silent or want an attorney
The pattern. Lying about facts (evidence, witnesses, identity) is generally allowed. Lying about consequences (promising a benefit or threatening harm) is not.

LEARNFive common interrogation arenas

The same rules apply differently depending on where the questioning happens.

ArenaThe rule from the notes
On school groundsAdministrators have lower search rules than police, but police must still respect your right to remain silent
Traffic stopsOfficers can stop you for a real, minor offense. You owe ID, registration and insurance. They cannot stop you just to check for drugs without a warrant

LEARNHow to invoke your rights

Knowing the rules does not help if you do not know the exact move that stops the questions. It is a learnable script.

  1. Police questioning begins.
  2. Say this clearly: "I am invoking my right to remain silent, and I want a lawyer."
  3. Stop talking. Do not explain, guess or make small talk. Even small talk can be used later.
  4. Once you have said it clearly, all legal questioning must stop.
The part almost everyone gets wrong. Saying it once does not protect you forever if you then start talking again. You can invoke your rights at any point, even mid-interrogation, but you can also un-invoke them by choosing to keep answering questions.

LEARNWhy short is safest

Everything else, such as explaining, arguing or "just clearing things up," gives an interrogation more material to work with. One clear sentence gives it none.

What you might doWhat it gives the interrogation
Explain your sideNew statements that can be compared with the evidence
Argue with the officerAnger and details that can be used later
Make small talkA relationship, and things said that can be quoted
Say the sentence and stopNothing
This is not about being guilty or innocent. Innocent people can be tricked into saying things that sound bad. The sentence protects everyone the same way.

LEARNSaying no to a search

The same idea works for what you hand over. An officer asking politely is not a warrant. You can calmly say "I do not consent to a search." Saying it is not rude and it is not a sign of guilt. It keeps the question about whether the police have a warrant or another legal reason, and it is the right the 4th Amendment gives you.

Rules differ by state and situation, and this guide is a classroom introduction, not legal advice. If you are ever questioned for real, the safest steps are the ones in this lesson: stay calm, say the sentence, stop talking, and get a lawyer.

LEARNEntropy and brute force

Entropy measures how unpredictable a password is. More possible characters and more length both raise entropy. Entropy, not looking complicated, decides how many guesses an attacker needs.

A brute-force attack tries every possible combination, one at a time, until one works. Short passwords fall fast because there are not many combinations, and each extra character multiplies the total.

PasswordRough entropy (bits)What it means
8 random characters from letters, digits and symbols, such as Qx7#vLm2about 53Needs a very large number of guesses
4 random words, such as river-comet-anchor-mapleabout 52About as strong as 8 random characters, and easier to remember
5 random wordsabout 65Far stronger than 8 random characters
An 8-letter lowercase wordabout 38Small number of guesses
Each extra bit doubles the number of guesses. Length from randomness beats clever symbols. The numbers above are from the course's password lab, which assumes an attacker who stole a site's password file and can try 10 billion guesses a second.

LEARNDictionary attacks and leetspeak

Instead of guessing randomly, a dictionary attack starts from real words and leaked password lists and applies predictable tweaks: a capital letter, 123 on the end, symbols swapped in. It is much faster than brute force against any password built on a real word.

Leetspeak swaps look-alike characters, like @ for a or 0 for o, so P@ssw0rd! looks complicated. Cracking tools check these swaps automatically, so it barely slows an attacker down. In the lab, P@ssw0rd! is "cracked instantly."

A password that looks clever to a person is not a password that is hard for a machine.

LEARNHashes and rainbow tables

Sites do not store your actual password. They store a scrambled version called a hash. If your password is common, an attacker can pre-compute the hashes of common passwords into a lookup chart, a rainbow table, and find yours instantly.

IdeaWhat it means for you
The site stores a hash, not your passwordA breach of the site's list does not directly show your password
The hash of a common password is already knownIf your password is common, the hash is no protection
A long random password is not in any tableLength and randomness protect you here too

LEARNCredential stuffing and keyloggers

Credential stuffing: when one site is breached, attackers try that same username and password on every other site they can think of. Reusing a password turns a single leak into access to all of your accounts.

A keylogger is software that secretly records every key you press, including passwords, and sends the log to an attacker. No password is strong enough to beat a keylogger, because the attacker never has to guess. They read what you typed.

AttackThe only real answer
Credential stuffingA different password for every site
KeyloggerKeep devices clean, and use MFA so a stolen password alone does not work

LEARNWhat copyright covers

You do not apply for copyright and you do not need a (c) symbol. The exact moment you make something, such as writing, filming, recording or saving it, it is automatically protected. No form, no fee and no waiting period.

ProtectedNot protected
A song you recordedFacts (the sky is blue)

An essay, story or poemTitles, names or short phrases

Copyright protects the exact thing you made, the specific words, notes or pixels, not the idea behind it. Two people can each write their own story about a vampire falling in love. It becomes a problem only if one copies the other's exact sentences.

LEARNThe five exclusive rights

RightWhat it means

LEARNThe fair use test

Fair use lets you use a piece of someone else's work without asking, but only if it passes a four-part test. Courts and platforms weigh all four together. No single one automatically wins.

FactorThe question
1. Why are you using it?Is it transformative: your own commentary, joke, lesson or review? Or is it a plain repost for views?
2. What kind of work is it?Factual work (news, data) gets more leeway than highly creative work (a song, a movie)
3. How much did you use?A 5-second clip to make a point is much safer than the whole song, scene or article
4. Does it hurt sales?Could someone watch your version instead of paying for the real thing? This is the factor courts and platforms weigh heaviest

LEARNPublic domain and Creative Commons

Some work is free to use because the copyright expired. Other work is free because the creator chose to give some rights away.

Public domain means nobody owns the copyright. In the U.S. this usually happens automatically to very old work (in 2026, generally anything published in 1930 or earlier; the cutoff moves forward one year every January 1), or when a creator gives up all rights. Anything made by the U.S. federal government, like NASA photos, is public domain.

LicenseWhat you must doExample

"Royalty-free" does not mean free to use. It usually means you pay once instead of every time. Read the actual license.

LEARNEvery crime has a category

Almost no crime is one single charge. The law breaks crimes into categories based on intent, planning and harm, and the category changes the penalty. Killing someone is not automatically murder. Stealing something is not automatically grand theft.

CategoryDescription

Second-degree murderIntentional but not planned; the intent formed in the moment

CategoryDescription

LEARNWhat to do instead

If you find a weakness, there is a safe path.

  1. Stop. Do not go further to confirm it.
  2. Write down what you saw and how, without exploring more.
  3. Find the owner's official way to report problems, such as a security contact or a program that invites researchers.
  4. If there is no official way, report it to a teacher or another responsible adult.
  5. Do not share the weakness publicly or try it on other sites.
Real security work is built on the opposite habit from curiosity: ask first, in writing, then test only what you were allowed to test.

LEARNHTTPS: the lock on the connection

Plain HTTP, with no S, sends everything you type, such as your password, your messages and your searches, as plain readable text, like writing on a postcard. Anyone else on the same network can read it as it travels. HTTPS is the same thing locked in a sealed envelope first.

What the padlock checksWhat it does

The padlock is not a "trust me" badge. HTTPS only proves nobody in the middle can read your connection. It says nothing about whether the site is honest. A scammer can build a perfect copy of your bank's login page at a look-alike address, and it can have a valid padlock too.
