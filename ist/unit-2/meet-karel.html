Meet Karel

Everything here shows up starting in 2.1 — read this first if Karel or the () after every command is confusing you.

What is Karel?

Karel is a friendly virtual dog (yes, dog) who lives on a grid world made of squares. She can walk around, pick up objects called beepers, and put them back down. That's it — and that's the point. Karel can't do anything you don't explicitly tell her to do — no guessing, no assuming, no "figuring it out." Every single move has to be spelled out in code, one instruction at a time. That's the whole point: Karel is how you practice thinking the way a computer needs you to think before you move on to real programming languages.

Where did Karel come from?

Karel was invented in the late 1970s by Richard Pattis at Stanford University. She was named after Karel Čapek, the Czech author who first used the word "robot" in a 1920 play. Since then, millions of students worldwide have learned to code by teaching Karel to move.

"The best way to learn programming is to start simple — and Karel keeps it beautifully simple."

Karel's World

Imagine a grid like a city map. Karel always faces one of four directions: North, South, East, or West. Squares on the grid can contain walls (obstacles) or beepers (items to collect). Karel's job? Follow your instructions to navigate the grid and solve puzzles.

  • Streets run left-to-right (East/West). Avenues run up-and-down (North/South).
  • Karel always starts at a specific square, facing a specific direction.
  • "Beepers" are small markers Karel can carry in her beeper bag — puzzles may require picking them up, moving them, or placing them in exact spots.

Karel's Basic Movements

These four are all Karel knows how to do at the start — everything else in this unit (functions, loops, if statements) is just you combining these four in smarter ways.

CommandWhat it does
move()Moves Karel forward one block in the direction it's currently facing.
turnLeft()Turns Karel 90° to the left. Karel can't turn right directly — you have to call turnLeft() three times to fake it (that's the whole point of 2.3).
putBeeper()Puts down a beeper at Karel's current location.
pickBeeper()Picks up a beeper at Karel's current location.
Later on (2.9, "Super Karel") you'll get two bonus commands built in for free: turnRight() and turnAround(). Until then, if you need to turn right, you build it yourself out of three turnLeft() calls — see 2.3.

Why does move() have ()?

move by itself is just a name — it's the name of an action Karel knows how to perform. Typing the name alone doesn't make anything happen, the same way saying the word "jump" out loud doesn't make you jump.

The parentheses () are what actually runs it. They tell the computer "stop just talking about this action — do it, right now." This is called calling the function. Every single command Karel understands — move(), turnLeft(), putBeeper(), pickBeeper() — needs those parentheses at the end, every time, or it won't run.

If you ever forget the (), your program won't crash loudly — it'll just silently do nothing for that line. If Karel isn't moving and you don't know why, that's the first thing to check.

So what goes inside the parentheses?

For move(), turnLeft(), putBeeper(), and pickBeeper() — nothing. They're empty on purpose, because Karel doesn't need any extra information to know what to do; "move forward one block" is the whole instruction.

Later in this unit you'll meet commands that do need extra information, and that's exactly where it goes — inside the parentheses. For example, a for loop like for i in range(4): uses the parentheses to tell the computer how many times to repeat. Same parentheses, same idea: it's where you hand the function whatever details it needs to do its job.

A quick example

Suppose Karel starts facing East and needs to move forward three squares, then pick up a beeper. Here's what that program looks like:

def pick_up_beeper():
    move()          # step 1
    move()          # step 2
    move()          # step 3
    pickBeeper()    # grab the beeper!

Simple, right? But now imagine Karel has to navigate a maze with walls, pick up beepers in unpredictable spots, and handle situations she's never seen before. Suddenly you're writing real logic — loops, if statements, and reusable functions. By the time you finish this unit, you'll already think like a programmer.

Why do we learn programming with Karel?

No syntax headaches

When you're just starting out, error messages about missing colons or wrong variable types get frustrating fast. Karel's world is stripped down so you can focus on thinking like a programmer instead of fighting the language.

Real programming concepts

Don't let the simplicity fool you. You'll use loops, conditionals, and functions — the exact same building blocks used in Python, Java, and every other language. Karel teaches the thinking, not just the typing.

Immediate satisfaction

Watching Karel walk across the screen and solve a puzzle you designed is genuinely fun. You can see your code work in real time — that feedback loop builds confidence and keeps you motivated to tackle harder problems.

Problem decomposition

Karel puzzles train you to break big problems into small steps — a skill professional developers use every single day. If you can plan Karel's path across a complicated maze, you can plan a real-world algorithm.

The language is just syntax. The thinking is everything.
