Meet Karel

Everything here shows up starting in 2.1 — read this first if Karel or the () after every command is confusing you.

What is Karel?

Karel is a virtual robot that lives on a grid made of streets and avenues. Karel can't do anything you don't explicitly tell it to do — no guessing, no assuming, no "figuring it out." Every single move has to be spelled out in code, one instruction at a time. That's the whole point: Karel is how you practice thinking the way a computer needs you to think before you move on to real programming languages.

Karel's World

  • Karel's world is a grid of streets (running left-right) and avenues (running up-down), sometimes with walls blocking a path.
  • Karel always faces one direction: North, South, East, or West.
  • "Beepers" are the little tennis balls Karel can pick up and put down on the grid.

Karel's Basic Movements

These four are all Karel knows how to do — everything else in this unit (functions, loops, if statements) is just you combining these four in smarter ways.

CommandWhat it does
move()Moves Karel forward one block in the direction it's currently facing.
turnLeft()Turns Karel 90° to the left. Karel can't turn right directly — you have to call turnLeft() three times to fake it (that's the whole point of 2.3).
putBeeper()Puts down a beeper at Karel's current location.
pickBeeper()Picks up a beeper at Karel's current location.

Why does move() have ()?

move by itself is just a name — it's the name of an action Karel knows how to perform. Typing the name alone doesn't make anything happen, the same way saying the word "jump" out loud doesn't make you jump.

The parentheses () are what actually runs it. They tell the computer "stop just talking about this action — do it, right now." This is called calling the function. Every single command Karel understands — move(), turnLeft(), putBeeper(), pickBeeper() — needs those parentheses at the end, every time, or it won't run.

If you ever forget the (), your program won't crash loudly — it'll just silently do nothing for that line. If Karel isn't moving and you don't know why, that's the first thing to check.

So what goes inside the parentheses?

For move(), turnLeft(), putBeeper(), and pickBeeper() — nothing. They're empty on purpose, because Karel doesn't need any extra information to know what to do; "move forward one block" is the whole instruction.

Later in this unit you'll meet commands that do need extra information, and that's exactly where it goes — inside the parentheses. For example, a for loop like for i in range(4): uses the parentheses to tell the computer how many times to repeat. Same parentheses, same idea: it's where you hand the function whatever details it needs to do its job.
