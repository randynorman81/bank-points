Honey Bee

The honey bee is a flying insect that lives in large family groups called colonies. There are several species of honey bee, but the one most people picture is the Western honey bee, whose scientific name is Apis mellifera. Honey bees are known around the world for making honey and for helping plants make fruit and seeds.

A single colony can hold between twenty thousand and sixty thousand bees. Nearly every bee in the colony is a worker. Workers are all female, and they do almost every job in the hive, from cleaning and feeding young bees to guarding the entrance and collecting food. Each colony also has one queen and, during warm months, several hundred male bees called drones.

The queen is the only bee in the colony that lays eggs. In the busiest part of the summer she can lay well over a thousand eggs in a single day. She is larger than the workers, and the workers feed and protect her constantly. If the queen dies or stops laying, the colony raises a new queen from a young female larva by feeding her a special food called royal jelly.

Honey bees build their homes out of beeswax. Workers produce small flakes of wax from glands on their bodies and shape them into rows of six-sided cells called honeycomb. The cells are used to store honey and pollen and to raise young bees. The six-sided shape uses very little wax and holds a great deal, which is one reason the honeycomb is so strong.

Worker bees visit flowers to collect nectar, a sugary liquid, and pollen, a powder that gives the colony protein. Back at the hive, the bees pass nectar from mouth to mouth. They add special substances from their bodies and fan it with their wings until most of the water has evaporated. The thick liquid that is left is honey. One worker will make only about a twelfth of a teaspoon of honey in her whole life.

Bees have an amazing way of telling each other where the best flowers are. A worker who finds a good patch returns to the hive and performs a movement called the waggle dance. The angle of the dance shows the direction of the flowers compared with the sun, and the length of the waggle shows how far away they are. The scientist Karl von Frisch studied this dance and shared a Nobel Prize in 1973 for his discoveries about animal behavior.

Honey bees keep the inside of their hive at about thirty-five degrees Celsius, which is close to ninety-five degrees Fahrenheit. On hot days they fan their wings and bring in water to cool the hive. In winter they gather into a tight ball called a cluster and shiver their flight muscles to make heat. The bees on the outside of the cluster slowly trade places with the bees in the warm center.

Honey bees are important to people because they pollinate many crops. When a bee moves from flower to flower, pollen sticks to her body and is carried to the next flower, which lets the plant make seeds and fruit. Farmers often rent hives during the spring so that crops such as apples, almonds, and blueberries are pollinated. Many of the foods in a grocery store depend at least partly on bees.

People have kept bees for thousands of years. Paintings in ancient Egypt show beekeepers working with smoke and clay hives, and honey was used as food and as a sweetener long before sugar was common. Today a beekeeper uses a wooden box with movable frames, which makes it possible to check the colony and collect honey without destroying the hive.

Honey bees face several serious threats. A tiny parasite called the varroa mite attacks bees and spreads disease inside the colony. Some pesticides harm bees, and the loss of wildflower habitat means there is less food to find. In some years beekeepers have reported losing a large share of their colonies over the winter, which worries farmers and scientists alike.

There are many ways to help honey bees. People can plant flowers that bloom at different times of the year, avoid using strong pesticides in gardens, and leave a shallow dish of water outside for thirsty insects. Buying honey from local beekeepers also supports people who take care of colonies. Even small changes in a yard or a school garden can give bees the food and safe places they need.

Fun fact: a honey bee visits dozens of flowers on a single trip, and the bees in a hive together fly a distance often estimated at about twice around the Earth to make one pound of honey.
