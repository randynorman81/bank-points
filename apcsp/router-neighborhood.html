Your Router Is a Neighborhood

Picture your router as a neighborhood. One road leads in from the Internet. Inside, every device is a house with its own private house number. People driving past do not see which house is which.

But the moment a device sends something out, it leaves the neighborhood and crosses networks you do not control. People along the way could see it unless it is encrypted (that is what HTTPS does).
01

Private and Public Addresses

A private address only works inside your neighborhood. A public address is unique on the whole Internet.

Private addressPublic address
Who can reach itonly devices on the same home or school networkanyone on the Internet

Can two homes use the same one?Yes. House number 20 exists on many streets.No. It must be unique.
02

NAT: The Gatehouse

NAT (Network Address Translation) lets a whole home share one public address. When a device sends something out, the router swaps its private address for the public one and remembers who asked. When the answer comes back, the router looks up who asked and delivers it to the right house.

Click through: how NAT sends a request out and brings the reply back

The laptop sends a request. It uses its private address as the source.
The router rewrites the source to its own public address and writes a note in the NAT table so it remembers who asked.
The server sends the reply to the router's public address. It has no idea the laptop exists.
The router checks its table and passes the reply to the right house.
The Internet only ever sees the router. This also helped slow the running-out of IPv4 addresses, because thousands of homes can reuse the same private numbers.
03

DHCP: The Moving Truck

How does a new device get its house number? DHCP (Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol). When a device joins your Wi-Fi, the router automatically assigns it the next free private address, like a moving truck handing you the keys to the next empty house. You never type an address yourself.

Try it: DHCP hands out house numbers

The router keeps a pool of private addresses. Click a device to make it join the Wi-Fi (it gets the next free address) or leave (the address goes back to the pool).

Lease: the address is only borrowed for a while. When the lease ends or the device leaves, the address can go to someone else.
04

Check yourself

Answer each one. You see why right away, so read the explanation even when you are right.

What does NAT let a home do?

NAT swaps private addresses for the router's one public address, so many devices share it.

What does DHCP do?

DHCP assigns addresses. DNS (next lesson) is the one that translates names.

Two different homes both have a laptop at 192.168.1.20. Is that a problem?

Private addresses are reused by thousands of networks. Only public addresses must be unique.

A server sends a reply to your home. Which address does it send the reply to?

The server never saw the private address. It replies to the router's public address, and NAT delivers it.
AP

On the AP Exam

These are the ideas the exam asks about. Be ready to explain each one in your own words.

  • A router connects a home network to the Internet and shares the connection.
  • Private addresses work only inside a network and can be reused. A public address is unique on the Internet.
  • NAT lets many private devices share one public address.
  • DHCP automatically assigns a device a (temporary) private address.
  • Data sent over a network you do not control can be seen unless it is encrypted.
