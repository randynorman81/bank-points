Who Is in the Data?

Conclusions are only as good as the data behind them, and data is only as good as how it was collected. The population is everyone you want to learn about. The sample is the part you actually collect data from. If the sample does not represent the population, the conclusions can be wrong.

Try it: ask 100 students a question

A school of 1,000 students: 500 eat first lunch (20% prefer online school) and 500 eat second lunch (70% prefer it). The truth for the whole school is about 45%. How you pick who to ask changes what you find.

01

Common Ways Data Becomes Biased

Bias can sneak in before any computer is involved.

KindWhat goes wrongExample
Sampling biasthe people asked are not typical of everyonepolling only people who have landlines
Self-selection biaspeople choose whether to take part, and the choosers differan online poll where angry people reply far more
Survivorship biasyou only see what "survived"studying only the companies that still exist, then concluding all startups succeed
Missing groupssome people cannot be reached at allan app-based survey misses people with no smartphone (the digital divide)
A bigger sample does not fix a biased method. Asking 10,000 people at the same lunch table still tells you about that table.
02

Biased Data, Biased Results

A system trained or tested on biased data gives biased results. This is how computing bias often begins, which is the next idea in the Impact of Computing notes.

1

Ask who is missing

Who might not be in this data? Who is over-represented?

2

Ask how it was collected

Random, voluntary, convenient? Was anyone pressured to answer?

3

Ask what was measured

Is the number really the thing you care about, or only something close to it?

03

Trust, Consent, and Privacy

Collecting data about people raises ethical questions as well as technical ones.

QuestionWhy it matters
Did people agree (consent) to have their data collected?People have a right to know what is collected and why
Is the data anonymous, and is that really true?Small facts can combine to identify someone (see privacy and PII)
Who can see and sell it?Data can be sold, leaked, or used in ways no one expected
Will it be used fairly?Decisions about loans, jobs, or schools made from biased data can harm people

To learn what all students at a school think about the lunch menu, a student surveys only the students sitting at one lunch table. What is the main problem?
The people asked are not typical of everyone: sampling bias.
A website runs an optional poll, and mostly people with strong opinions respond. This is an example of…
People choose whether to take part, and the choosers differ from everyone else.
A researcher says: "My biased survey is fine because I asked 50,000 people." What is wrong with that?
Method matters more than size.
A study of successful companies concludes that quitting college makes you rich, because it only looked at famous founders who did. This is…
It ignores everyone who did the same thing and did not succeed.
AP

On the AP Exam

These are the ideas the exam asks about. Be ready to explain each one in your own words.

  • A sample should represent the population. Biased sampling gives biased conclusions.
  • Know sampling bias, self-selection bias, and survivorship bias. A bigger sample does not fix a biased method.
  • Biased data leads to biased results from programs that use it.
  • Data collection raises questions of consent, privacy, and fairness.
